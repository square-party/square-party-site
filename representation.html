The Foundation

The Numbers Don't Work

When the Constitution was written, one member of Congress represented about 30,000 people. George Washington thought that number was already too high — it was the only thing he spoke up about during the entire Constitutional Convention.

Today, one House member represents roughly 761,000 people.

That's not a partisan problem. It's a math problem. No human being can meaningfully understand the needs, priorities, and daily realities of three-quarters of a million people. It doesn't matter how talented or well-intentioned they are. The information load is simply beyond what one person can process across dozens of policy areas. Your representative is not failing you because they're corrupt or lazy — they're failing you because the job is structurally impossible at this scale.

The Core Ask

You're the Missing Piece

Here's what most reform proposals get wrong: they start with the fix. More seats in Congress. New districts. New rules. But that skips the most important step — actually understanding what people need.

The government is supposed to represent the will of the people. That means it's supposed to be guided, directed, and maintained by the people. Right now, your representative probably doesn't know what you think about housing policy, or water infrastructure, or how federal education funding affects your kids' school. Not because they don't care. Because there's no system for asking you, and no system for listening to 761,000 answers.

We want to build that system. And it starts with something simple: organizing people around the issues they actually care about.

We're not asking you to become an activist or join a movement. We're asking you to raise your hand on one thing — one issue, one area where you have an opinion or some experience — and let your voice be counted. That's it. That's the foundation.

The Proposal

What This Looks Like in Practice

Picture your congressional district divided not by geography, but by what people care about. Twenty to thirty groups, each organized around a policy area — healthcare, housing, education, criminal justice, agriculture, energy, veterans' affairs, and so on. Each group has a coordinator. Each coordinator's job is simple: collect opinions from the people in that group before any relevant vote or proposal, and make sure the representative sees the results.

This isn't hypothetical. This is how politics used to work at the local level in America. Before the professionalization of campaigns, ward leaders and precinct captains knew their neighbors and carried their concerns directly to elected officials. The structure worked — what failed was the incentive system around it, not the idea of local organization itself.

The modern version is better. We have tools the ward captains didn't: surveys that take two minutes on your phone, platforms that can map where opinion clusters and where it divides, and transparency tools that let everyone see the results. A representative who adopts this model doesn't just claim to represent their district. They can show it — with data, in real time.

Any sitting representative or any candidate can do this tomorrow. No new laws. No constitutional amendments. No permission from anyone. Just a decision to actually ask the people what they think, organized well enough that the answers are useful.

The Structural Argument

But We Actually Need More Representatives

Organizing within existing districts is the starting point, not the destination. Even with perfect constituent feedback, one person still can't do the job of meaningful representation for 761,000 people.

The United States House of Representatives has had 435 members since 1929. That number wasn't chosen because it's the right size for a democracy. It was frozen in place because rural states in the 1920s didn't want to lose seats to fast-growing cities after the Census. That's it. The cap is a political accident that's almost a hundred years old.

Political scientists who study legislatures around the world have found that effective representative bodies tend to grow with population — specifically, they tend to follow a "cube root" pattern, where the legislature is roughly the cube root of the country's population. For the U.S. today, that math points to somewhere around 650 to 700 members. Not 10,000. Not 3,000. A few hundred more than we have now.

That's a modest change with enormous consequences. Smaller districts mean representatives who can actually know their communities. It means more voices in the room when policy gets made. And it means the constituent organizing system described above gets even more powerful — because the groups are smaller, the feedback loops are tighter, and the connection between citizens and their government gets real.

The Bigger Vision

The People's Assembly

Even 700 House members won't bring representation back to the scale the founders intended. To do that — to create a layer of government where your representative actually knows your neighborhood — you'd need something new.

One version of that idea: a People's Assembly. Not a replacement for Congress, but a layer beneath it. Think of it as a formalized, permanent version of the constituent organizing system — built into the structure of government rather than depending on the goodwill of individual representatives.

Representatives in the People's Assembly would each serve roughly 100,000 constituents — about the size of a small city or a few towns. That's approximately 3,300 members nationwide. They would serve short terms — one or two years — to stay close to the people they represent. They'd handle issues that most directly affect daily life: local infrastructure, housing, education funding, community services. Most of their work would happen through digital platforms, with periodic in-person assemblies.

The key structural feature: all legislation would pass through the People's Assembly as a "first reading" before reaching the House. Not as a veto — as a filter. A way of making sure that the people who will be affected by a law have had a structured opportunity to weigh in before it moves forward.

This isn't Congress 2.0. It's closer to a national version of the New England town meeting — one of the oldest democratic traditions in America. The difference is scale, and the tools to make it work at scale now exist.

The Evidence

This Has Been Tried — and It Works

The idea of giving citizens a structured voice in policy isn't new, and it isn't foreign. American cities have been doing versions of this for years. Participatory budgeting, citizens' assemblies, and structured deliberation have been tested in real governments, with real results. The consistent finding: when you give regular people real information and real deliberation time, they produce thoughtful, nuanced policy recommendations — often more thoughtful than what legislatures produce, because the participants aren't performing for donors or media.

The tools exist. The evidence exists. What's missing is the decision to use them — and that decision can start with one representative, one candidate, or one district that decides to try.
