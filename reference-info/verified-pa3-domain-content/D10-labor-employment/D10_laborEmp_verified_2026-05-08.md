## Synthesis Section 1: What This Domain Established

The seven sub-domains of D10 Labor & Employment document the legal and programmatic architecture governing the employer-employee relationship and labor market participation in PA-3 — a regulatory architecture that is, at the formal level, jurisdictionally dense, multi-layered, and in significant respects more protective at the local level than the federal baseline, and that produces, at the level of actual benefit reaching the PA-3 worker population, systematically attenuated outcomes traceable to specific design features of the multi-jurisdictional system. The findings below are stated at the confidence level the underlying SD evidence supports; each is labeled per Standard 5.A by evidence type ([D] documented, [SD] structurally derived, [SI] structural inference) and confidence (HIGH, MEDIUM, LOW). Per-SD findings appear first; cross-cutting findings derived from the substructure §7 analytical threads (D10-Thread A, B, C; DOMAIN_FRAMEWORK Threads 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7) follow.

### Per-Sub-Domain Findings

**Finding 1 (SD1 — Labor Market Infrastructure & Governance). [SD] HIGH for the structural existence of the navigation problem; MEDIUM for PA-3-specific magnitude pending F-flag retrieval.** The legal architecture governing labor market governance in PA-3 — federal (WHD, OSHA, ETA, OFCCP, NLRB Region 4, EEOC), state (PA L&I/BLLC, PLRB, PHRC), and local (PDOL, PCHR, Philadelphia Works) — is formally comprehensive and structurally produces a first-point-of-contact navigation burden that falls disproportionately on workers without legal representation, English-language access, or prior system exposure. The navigation burden is a logically necessary structural consequence of overlapping multi-jurisdictional design with different statutes of limitation, employer-size thresholds, remedies, and procedural pathways. Two further architectural findings: (a) PDOL, PCHR, and PA BLLC operate with structurally constrained staffing relative to the covered employer universe in PA-3 (capacity-to-universe ratio Phase 3-flagged at F10-SD1-08); (b) OFCCP's EO 11246 enforcement at PA-3 anchor employers has been eliminated as a practical matter following EO 14173 (January 21, 2025), which revoked EO 11246 entirely and reduced OFCCP from ~479 staff across 55 offices to ~50 staff in 4 regional locations (T10-SD1-01; MC-01; see SD5 for full analysis). SD1 documents D10-Thread A (formal-program-to-actual-benefit gap) at the governance-architecture level as the jurisdictional-fragmentation finding.

**Finding 2 (SD2 — Employment Standards). [D]/[SD] HIGH for the structural pattern; MEDIUM for PA-3-specific magnitude across the Both/And layered preemption finding; LOW for D10-Q2 wage theft scale absent further retrieval.** The federal/state/local employment-standards architecture for PA-3 workers is layered and substantively protective in several dimensions (Philadelphia's Wage Theft Ordinance, Fair Workweek Ordinance, Domestic Workers Bill of Rights, and Sick Leave Ordinance constitute among the most protective municipal employment-standards architectures in the country), AND the most consequential single lever — the wage floor — is preempted at the local level by 43 P.S. § 333.115 (PMWA, added by Act 1 of 2006), locking PA-3's lowest-wage workers at the federal $7.25 floor whose real value has eroded substantially since the 2009 last federal increase. The Both/And layered-preemption finding (substructure §6; MC28) operates as: substantive protections that have not been preempted (sick leave per Pittsburgh / Philadelphia line of cases; Fair Workweek; DWBOR) are real provisions with real enforcement infrastructure; the wage floor preemption is the structural design feature that locates PA-3's wage-adequacy gap at the level of state law rather than at the federal or local levels where representation has more direct purchase. Three further structural findings at SD2: (a) FMLA's 50-employee and 12-month thresholds exclude the workers who most need paid leave (G10-SD2-03 paid family leave structural coverage gap); (b) wage theft enforcement architecture is capacity-constrained and mechanism-fragmented, with the small-LLC judgment-collection gap producing a structural environment where wage theft is low-risk for non-compliant employers in PA-3's food-service and informal-construction sectors (G10-SD2-04; D10-Q2 held-open at magnitude per Phase 3 three-search budget disposition — MC-07); (c) FLSA-side worker misclassification (economic-reality test) is the analytical center of D10-Thread C cross-test misclassification phenomenon (G10-SD2-05). SD2 documents D10-Thread A as the wage-floor preemption finding and D10-Thread C primary placement.

**Finding 3 (SD3 — Workplace Safety & Workers' Compensation). [D]/[SD] HIGH for the formal framework comprehensiveness; MEDIUM for the misclassification-gateway gap magnitude; the heat-illness administrative-vulnerability finding LOW pending final-rule disposition.** The OSH Act and PA Workers' Compensation Act provide a comprehensively designed occupational protection framework for PA-3's formally employed workforce — General Duty Clause, specific standards (construction, healthcare, HazCom, bloodborne pathogens, lead in construction), inspection and citation authority, OSHA whistleblower protection, no-fault workers' compensation with medical treatment without deductible — AND four structural gaps concentrate at the boundary between formal employment and informal/subcontracted work: (a) federal OSHA's Philadelphia Area Office covers a large geographic area with limited inspector staff, producing low effective inspection probability for informal construction concentrated in PA-3 (G10-SD3-01); (b) the PA WCA misclassification gateway (multi-factor test) excludes misclassified workers from no-fault injury coverage entirely, creating a multi-system exclusion (FLSA SD2 + NLRA SD4 + UC SD6 + WCA SD3) that compounds for the same worker across four legal frameworks (G10-SD3-02; D10-Thread C cross-test misclassification operationalization); (c) the panel-of-physicians provision in WCA gives employers control over the first 90 days of medical treatment after an injury, producing a structural information asymmetry against injured workers (G10-SD3-03); (d) the absence of a specific OSHA heat illness standard leaves heat-intensive occupations (construction; agriculture; warehouse without adequate cooling; restaurant kitchens) covered only under the General Duty Clause, with the 2024 OSHA heat illness rulemaking indefinitely stalled under the current administration (G10-SD3-04; T10-SD3-01; MC-04: post-hearing comment period closed October 30, 2025; no final rule target date; OSHA NEP set to expire April 8, 2026). The reciprocal cross-reference to D2 (Public Health, verified 2026-05-02) preserves the boundary: D2 owns occupational health pathway (lead-paint, asbestos, silicosis, heat illness, healthcare-sector COVID-19 exposure as health-outcome territory); D10 SD3 owns the regulatory framework. **Finding 6 (SD6 — Unemployment Compensation). [D]/[SD] HIGH for the structural framework architecture; D10-Q3 best-estimate framing operationalized at SD6 with PUA dataset empirical floor; MEDIUM for the duration/replacement-rate adequacy finding; CONFIRMED: PA UC maximum weekly benefit $605/week (frozen per solvency trigger; MC-05; Standard 17 applied).** The PA UC system provides meaningful income replacement for formally employed workers who meet eligibility requirements — approximately 50% wage replacement subject to the current maximum of $605/week (frozen under solvency trigger; PA Bulletin official notice effective January 1, 2026; Standard 17: prior estimate $854/week preserved as acknowledged prior-method baseline; current figure $605/week is the operative 2026 maximum; 3.2% solvency reduction also applies to all benefit payments), up to 26 weeks duration, with an accessible online filing process and an appeals architecture (Service Centers → Referee → Board of Review → Commonwealth Court → PA Supreme Court) that formally protects claimant rights at each level. The PA UC Trust Fund remains below solvency; current PA L&I forecasts project 10+ years to reach full solvency (MC-06); solvency measures operative including the $605/week benefit freeze and employer surcharges. For PA-3's formally employed healthcare, education, retail, and public-sector workforce, the system functions as designed. Three structural gaps constrain actual receipt: (a) the independent-contractor exclusion removes the safety net from PA-3's gig workforce — Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, Instacart, TaskRabbit, and platform-mediated delivery and personal-services workers — whose economic reality mirrors formal employment but whose legal classification under the PA UC ABC test (43 P.S. § 753) excludes them from coverage; the PUA program (CARES Act 2020–2021) quantified this exclusion with state-administrative-data quality, and PUA expiration in September 2021 restored the coverage gap (G10-SD6-01; D10-Q3 best-estimate framing per substructure §8; D10-Thread C UC-side); (b) the employer experience-rating contestation incentive structurally converts the UC system into an adversarial proceeding in which unrepresented workers face employer teams at Referee hearings — the willful-misconduct standard (43 P.S. § 802(e); *Navickas v. UCBR*) and the voluntary-quit good-cause requirement become the principal contestation grounds; CLS and PUP serve a fraction of the unrepresented population (G10-SD6-02); (c) UC benefit duration (26 weeks) and replacement rate (~50% AWW subject to $605/week maximum and 3.2% solvency reduction) may be structurally insufficient for PA-3's highest-unemployment sub-areas where unemployment spells commonly exceed 26 weeks (G10-SD6-03). PA UC Trust Fund post-COVID solvency status is a confirmed Standard 11 administrative-vulnerability finding (T10-SD6-01; MC-06; D9 fiscal-side cross-reference preserved). ## Section 2: Authority Chain

### Constitutional Foundation

Federal labor market regulation rests on three constitutional pillars. The **Commerce Clause** (U.S. Const. Art. I § 8 cl. 3) supplies authority for the Fair Labor Standards Act, the National Labor Relations Act, OSHA, and the anti-discrimination statutes — each premised on the regulation of employment as commerce. The **Spending Clause** (U.S. Const. Art. I § 8 cl. 1) is the basis for conditional federal grants to states for workforce development programs (WIOA), unemployment compensation systems, and vocational rehabilitation — programs where federal funding flows in exchange for compliance with federal requirements. The **13th and 14th Amendments** ground the anti-discrimination employment statutes (Title VII; ADEA; ADA Title I; PWFA; GINA) as expressions of Congress's power to enforce substantive equality norms.

Pennsylvania's labor regulatory authority rests on the state police power and **Art. III § 25 of the Pennsylvania Constitution**, which specifically authorizes the General Assembly to regulate labor — a textually explicit constitutional grant that underlies the Pennsylvania Minimum Wage Act, the Wage Payment and Collection Law, the PLRA, PERA, the PHRA, and the Pennsylvania Workers' Compensation Act.

Statutory stability: HIGH at the constitutional-basis level. The Commerce Clause and Spending Clause premises are settled doctrine. Pennsylvania's Art. III § 25 authority is not legally contested.

## Section 3: Pathway Tracing

**The PA-3 Labor Market Navigation Problem**

The SD1 representative pathway for PA-3 is not a single-agency claim pathway; it is the first-point-of-contact selection problem that precedes any substantive claim. A PA-3 worker experiencing a labor violation must first correctly identify which agency — among the federal WHD (FLSA), the PA BLLC (PMWA/WPCL), and the city PDOL (Philadelphia Wage Theft Ordinance) — handles which type of complaint. This identification step is not administratively trivial: the three agencies operate under overlapping but non-identical coverage, with different procedures, different remedies, and different statutes of limitations.

**The wage theft pathway as representative trace:**

*Triggering event:* A PA-3 worker's employer fails to pay agreed wages or withholds earned overtime.

*First-contact decision:* The worker must decide: PDOL (city enforcement of Wage Theft Ordinance), WHD (federal FLSA enforcement), or PA BLLC (state WPCL/PMWA enforcement). The three overlap in coverage for most PA-3 private-sector employers. The choice is not neutral:
- WHD enforces FLSA, which covers overtime and the $7.25 federal minimum wage; the PUMP Act for nursing mothers; FMLA. WHD has stronger nationwide back-wages recovery infrastructure and can recover back wages administratively.
- PA BLLC enforces the WPCL, which creates a private right of action for wage claims and includes a penalty provision for willful violations; enforcement is complaint-driven.
- PDOL enforces the Philadelphia Wage Theft Ordinance, which applies specifically to work performed in Philadelphia and includes a private right of action and administrative hearing process; staffing constraints limit proactive enforcement.

*Decision point 1 — English-language access:* None of the three agencies has uniform multilingual intake capacity. Workers whose primary language is not English face additional barriers at the first-contact step.

*Decision point 2 — Representation availability:* Workers with legal representation (e.g., through Community Legal Services' Wage Theft unit or the Philadelphia Unemployment Project) are navigated directly to the agency with the highest recovery probability for the specific violation. Workers without representation must self-identify the correct agency. The gap between represented and unrepresented workers in navigation success is documented in the advocacy literature but not precisely quantified for PA-3 [F10-SD1-09].

*Decision point 3 — Statute of limitations awareness:* WHD FLSA claims: 2-year SOL (3 years for willful violations); WPCL claims: 3-year SOL; Philadelphia Wage Theft Ordinance: 3-year SOL. Filing with the wrong agency after the shorter WHD SOL has run forecloses federal remedies.

*Pathway outcome:* Workers with representation, English-language access, and prior system exposure achieve substantially higher navigation success. The architecture is nominally comprehensive — three overlapping agencies all covering wage theft in PA-3 — but the multiplicity of first-point-of-contact options structurally concentrates navigation burden on the workers least equipped to bear it.

**The navigation problem applied to the full agency architecture:**

The wage-theft pathway is illustrative of a broader pattern. Across D10's substantive regimes, the same first-contact selection problem recurs:
- Discrimination claims: EEOC (federal; Title VII 15+ employee threshold; 300-day filing deadline for PA employers) vs. PHRC (state; 4+ employee threshold; 180-day filing deadline) vs. PCHR (local; 1+ employee threshold; different procedures).
- Workplace safety violations: federal OSHA (private sector) vs. PA OSHA (public sector only).
- Labor relations (union organizing): NLRB Region 4 (private sector, above commerce thresholds) vs. PLRB (public sector; small private employers below NLRB threshold).

The first-point-of-contact navigation burden is the SD1 gap mechanism. It is a structurally traceable consequence of the multi-jurisdictional architecture's design.

## Section 6: Conversational Narrative Note

SD1 establishes the governance architecture for the domain — a structure that is, at its formal surface, impressively comprehensive. Federal enforcement (WHD, OSHA, ETA, OFCCP, NLRB Region 4, EEOC) interacts with state enforcement (PA L&I/BLLC, PLRB, PHRC) and local enforcement (PDOL, PCHR, Philadelphia Works) across a multi-layer architecture covering virtually every employment relationship in PA-3. The comprehensiveness is real — not a fictional formal provision — and SD1 is the place to establish that reality before subsequent SDs document where specific mechanisms produce gaps.

What SD1 surfaces is the architectural consequence of that very comprehensiveness: the existence of three overlapping agencies with jurisdiction over wage theft, or two over small-employer discrimination, produces a navigation burden that the architecture's designers did not architect away. The burden is not randomly distributed — it falls hardest on workers least served by legal representation: low-wage service workers in South/Southwest; reentry job-seekers in North/NW Core; immigrant workers facing English-language barriers; workers in informal employment.

The anchor institutions enter SD1 as the OFCCP-covered federal contractors whose employment footprint shapes the PA-3 labor market. Phase 3 verification has confirmed that the OFCCP's EO 11246 enforcement mechanism — which was the primary structural enforcement tool for addressing racial and gender discrimination at these large federal contractors — has been eliminated rather than merely reduced. Section 503 (disability) and VEVRAA (veterans) remain operative. The elimination of EO 11246 enforcement narrows the OFCCP channel for structural accountability at PA-3's anchor employers from three tracks (race/sex/national origin under EO 11246; disability under Section 503; veterans under VEVRAA) to two tracks (disability and veterans). This is SD1's governance-architecture-level documentation of what D10 SD5 analyzes at the substantive anti-discrimination level.

## Section 8: Representation Question Closure

**Representation Question for SD1 — Labor Market Infrastructure & Governance**

**Element 1 — Formal provision:** The legal architecture governing labor market governance in PA-3 is formally comprehensive. At the federal level: WHD, OSHA, ETA, OFCCP (now operating under reduced scope following EO 11246 revocation), NLRB Region 4, and EEOC address employment standards, safety, workforce development, contractor compliance, labor relations, and anti-discrimination respectively. Pennsylvania adds PA L&I/BLLC, PLRB, and PHRC with parallel and overlapping state-level jurisdiction. Philadelphia adds PDOL, PCHR, and Philadelphia Works. The formal provision is a jurisdiction-dense, multiply-layered governance architecture covering most employment relationships in PA-3 under multiple statutory protections.

**Element 2 — Actual receipt:** Access to the formal provision is uneven. Workers with legal representation are navigated effectively through the architecture. Workers without representation, facing English-language barriers, or in informal or misclassified employment relationships bear a structurally higher navigation burden at the first-contact selection step.

**Element 3 — Account for the gap:** The gap between formal provision and actual receipt is structurally traceable to the overlapping multi-jurisdictional architecture's design. Congress, the General Assembly, and the Philadelphia City Council each built enforcement mechanisms within their statutory scope, producing coverage-level comprehensiveness and worker-level navigational complexity. *Verification update (2026-05-08): The 2025–2026 administrative context adds a compounding structural gap: OFCCP's EO 11246 enforcement mechanism — the structural accountability track for race/sex/national origin discrimination at large federal contractors — has been eliminated by executive action, not merely constrained. This is not a navigational complexity problem (one of many agencies covering the same ground) but a structural removal (one enforcement mechanism for a specific accountability function eliminated entirely). See SD5 §7 G10-SD5-03 for full analysis. MC-01 secondary anchor.* The distributional pattern tracks the district's existing stratifications of representation, language access, and institutional experience.

**Element 4 — Meaning for representation analysis:** D10-Thread A (formal-program-to-actual-benefit gap) is named explicitly at SD1. SD1's contribution: the labor market governance architecture of PA-3 is comprehensive in formal coverage and complex in navigational architecture; the interaction of that complexity with the distribution of representation access produces a systematic gap between protection-on-paper and benefit-in-hand that tracks the district's racial and income stratification. The confirmed elimination of OFCCP EO 11246 enforcement is an additional structural dimension that narrows the accountability architecture for PA-3's largest employers.

### Pennsylvania Employment Standards

**Pennsylvania Minimum Wage Act (PMWA), 43 P.S. § 333.101 et seq.** PA minimum wage: $7.25/hour (matching federal floor; not raised since 2009). PA tipped minimum: $2.83/hour [F10-SD2-03: verify current PA tipped minimum at dli.pa.gov].

### Philadelphia Employment Standards

Enforcement: PDOL Office of Worker Protections.

**Philadelphia Domestic Workers Bill of Rights (DWBOR), Phila. Code Ch. 9-4500.** Enacted 2019. ## Section 6: Conversational Narrative Note

SD2's central analytical finding is that PA-3 has one of the most layered employment-standards architectures in the country at the local level — Fair Workweek, paid sick leave, Wage Theft Ordinance, Domestic Workers Bill of Rights — deployed in the space that the Pennsylvania preemption left open. The preemption is precise: it removed the wage floor, the single most consequential lever in employment standards law, and left the procedural, scheduling, and leave-related instruments intact. The result is an architecture that provides predictability pay to a Walmart worker whose schedule changed last-minute, but cannot raise that worker's base wage above a floor that was set in 2009 and has lost roughly 30% of its purchasing power in real terms since then.

The FMLA paid-leave gap is the domain where the federal floor is not itself a problem — FMLA is a meaningful protection for workers who can access it — but the 50-employee and 12-month thresholds exclude exactly the workers for whom leave protection would make the greatest practical difference: workers in the home care sector, in small restaurants, in domestic service, in informal construction.

The wage theft enforcement architecture illustrates the capacity-gap dimension of D10-Thread A. Three agencies — WHD, PA BLLC, PDOL — cover the same violations with overlapping jurisdiction, different procedures, and limited proactive enforcement capacity. D10-Q2 remains held-open at magnitude following Phase 3 retrieval; the mechanism is documented at HIGH confidence; the annual PA-3-scale dollar magnitude requires institutional retrieval.

### Pennsylvania Workers' Compensation Architecture

## Section 6: Conversational Narrative Note

SD4's structural finding is the subcontracting architecture as the mechanism that operationally creates the gap between anchor institution employers and the workers whose daily employment conditions they substantially shape. The formal provision — the NLRA framework — is working exactly as designed. The gap is that the design creates a structural distance between the institution with economic power (the university; its endowment; its federal contract revenue) and the legal employer (the service management company) whose workers are on the institutional premises every day.

The February 2026 NLRB final rule formally reinstating the narrow joint-employer standard closes the door on the one legal mechanism that could have required Penn to bargain directly over its subcontracted workforce's wages. The narrow standard — "direct and immediate control" — is not satisfied by Penn's contract pricing authority over Aramark, even though Penn's pricing decisions effectively determine the wages available in the Aramark contract.

The remedial structure compounds this gap. After 2–3 years of NLRB litigation following an organizing-related termination, the back-pay remedy may be a few thousand dollars. No punitive damages; no attorney's fees. The remedial structure does not deter employers with strong anti-union preferences.

**Element 1 — Formal provision:** The NLRA provides private-sector employees with legally enforceable rights to organize, form unions, and engage in collective bargaining. The representation analysis import: the largest private employers in PA-3 have structured their employment relationships to limit their direct accountability under the labor law system's most powerful mechanism for raising low-wage workers' compensation. ## Section 6: Conversational Narrative Note

SD5's analytical center is the mismatch between the instrument and the problem. Philadelphia has built among the most protective anti-discrimination legal architectures in the United States: PHRA covers employers down to 4 employees; PFPO covers employers down to 1; Ban the Box requires individualized assessment; PCHR is an active local FEPA. This is the Both/And's first half.

The gap is in the mechanism matching. The legal architecture — individual charge filing, investigation, conciliation, hearing — is designed to address intentional discrimination against identified individuals by identified employers. The dominant mechanism of racial employment disadvantage in PA-3 operates at a level that is invisible to individual charge architecture. A Black woman who sends résumés to 40 employers and is called back at half the rate of an equivalently qualified white woman cannot file a charge identifying the employer who discriminated, because she doesn't know — nobody called her.

## Section 2: Authority Chain

### Federal-State UC Framework

**Federal Unemployment Tax Act (FUTA), 26 U.S.C. § 3301 et seq.** Imposes federal payroll tax on employers: 6.0% of first $7,000 of each employee's wages per year, effectively reduced to 0.6% after credit for approved state programs. FUTA revenue funds federal administrative grants to states and the Federal Unemployment Trust Fund's loan account for state trust funds that become insolvent.

**Social Security Act Title III, 42 U.S.C. § 501 et seq.** Grants to states for UC administration, subject to federal standards.

**Social Security Act Title IX, 42 U.S.C. § 1101 et seq.** Federal Unemployment Trust Fund; federal extended benefits trigger mechanism.

### Pennsylvania Unemployment Compensation Law

**Pennsylvania UC Law, 43 P.S. § 751 et seq.** Pennsylvania designs its own UC system within federal minimum requirements.

*Coverage:* "Employment" broadly defined (43 P.S. § 753(l)). Excluded: self-employment; independent contractors; certain agricultural workers; certain domestic workers; certain religious organization employees; certain student workers. **The independent-contractor exclusion is the most consequential coverage boundary for PA-3's gig and informal workforce.**

*Eligibility requirements:*

1. **Base-year wage requirement (43 P.S. § 801):** Claimant must have earned sufficient wages in covered employment during the base year (first four of the last five completed calendar quarters — "regular base year"; alternatively, the last four completed calendar quarters — "alternative base year"). Pennsylvania requires wages in at least two quarters and total base-year wages sufficient to produce a weekly benefit rate. Prior-method figure of approximately $1,688 minimum base-year wages is a verification-flag item [F10-SD6-01: verify current PA UC base-year wage minimum at uc.pa.gov].

2. **Separation eligibility (43 P.S. § 802):** Claimant must be unemployed through no fault of their own. Primary disqualifying separations: voluntary quit without good cause attributable to the employer (§ 402(b)); discharge for willful misconduct (§ 402(e)); refusal of suitable work (§ 402(a)). Domestic violence as good cause: codified at 43 P.S. § 802(e)(3).

3. **Able and available (§ 801(d)(1)):** Claimant must be physically and mentally able to work, available for suitable work, and actively seeking employment.

*Weekly benefit amount and maximum — **MC-05 PRINCIPAL ANCHOR**:* Approximately 50% of the claimant's average weekly wage during the base year, subject to a maximum weekly benefit.

*Verification update (2026-05-08): **MC-05 PRINCIPAL ANCHOR.** The prior-method figure of $854/week cited in this OUTPUT as the PA UC maximum weekly benefit is a prior-method baseline figure; the current operative maximum weekly benefit is **$605/week**, effective January 1, 2026. The 3.2% solvency reduction applies to all benefit payments. The comparator: the current maximum represents approximately a 29% reduction from the prior-method figure, materially affecting the benefit-adequacy analysis in §7 G10-SD6-03. Sources 15 (PA Bulletin official notice, effective January 1, 2026).*

*Appeals architecture (five levels):*
1. Initial determination — UC Service Center.
2. **Referee hearing** — evidentiary hearing; critical evidentiary stage. Philadelphia Unemployment Project (PUP) provides advocacy and coaching; Community Legal Services (CLS) provides representation for income-eligible claimants.
3. UC Board of Review — typically decides on the record without a new hearing.
4. Commonwealth Court of Pennsylvania.
5. Pennsylvania Supreme Court.

*Employer experience-rating structure:* UC employer tax rates are experience-rated — employers with higher separation rates pay higher tax rates. This creates a **structural incentive for employers to contest UC claims** regardless of genuine eligibility concerns. The result: an adversarial claims process in which employers routinely contest claims driven by tax management. Unrepresented workers face employer HR departments or legal counsel at Referee hearings.

*PA UC ABC Test (worker misclassification — D10-Thread C tertiary):* PA UC applies a three-part "ABC test" for determining whether a worker is an employee (covered) or independent contractor (excluded): (A) free from control and direction in the performance of the service, both under the contract and in fact; AND (B) service is outside the usual course of business of the employer, or performed outside all employer places of business; AND (C) worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade, occupation, profession, or business. The ABC test is significantly **stricter for employers** than the FLSA economic-reality test (SD2) or the NLRA right-to-control test (SD4) — some workers classified as independent contractors and excluded from FLSA minimum wage or NLRA organizing rights may still be entitled to UC as employees under the ABC test. D10-Thread C: the three classification tests produce non-identical outcomes for the same worker; the ABC test provides the widest employee-protective classification standard in the misclassification cross-test.

## Section 6: Conversational Narrative Note

SD6 has the simplest analytical structure in this domain: the UC system functions as designed for formally employed workers meeting its eligibility requirements, and the gaps concentrate in two structural features that are themselves design choices. ## Section 2: Authority Chain

### Federal Workforce Development Framework

**Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA), 29 U.S.C. § 3101 et seq.** Enacted July 22, 2014; replaced the Workforce Investment Act of 1998. Spending Clause basis.

*Title I-B — Adult, Dislocated Worker, and Youth Programs:* Core employment and training services. Funding flow: DOL ETA → PA L&I → Philadelphia Works (Local WDB) → PA CareerLink Philadelphia service delivery sites. Three program streams: Adult Program (basic and individualized career services; training services; priority of service for low-income individuals); Dislocated Worker Program; Youth Program (in-school and out-of-school youth ages 14–24; 75% of youth funding for out-of-school youth per WIOA § 128(b)(4)).

*Individual Training Account (ITA):* The primary WIOA training funding mechanism for adults and dislocated workers. Philadelphia ITA maximum set by Philadelphia Works [F10-SD7-01: verify current Philadelphia ITA maximum at philaworks.org — prior-method range $6,000–$15,000/year]. The ITA maximum funds short-duration certifications (CNA, forklift operator, healthcare support, phlebotomy — 2–6 month durations) but is structurally insufficient to fund credential-length programs (associate degree at CCP; 2-year technical certificate; bachelor completion). This ITA cap-vs.-credential-threshold gap is SD7's primary Both/And tension.

*Title II — Adult Education and Literacy:* Grants to states for adult education, literacy, and English language acquisition services. D11 boundary: D10 SD7 references adult education at the workforce-development employment interface; D11 will own adult education program operation.

*Title III — Wagner-Peyser Act Services:* Employment services integrated into one-stop CareerLink system; job matching, labor exchange, referral to training.

*Title IV — Vocational Rehabilitation:* PA Office of Vocational Rehabilitation (OVR). Serves individuals with disabilities to achieve employment outcomes.

*Statutory stability:* HIGH. Administrative vulnerability: MODERATE-HIGH — WIOA funding levels are an annual appropriations decision; Trump administration has proposed budget reductions to workforce development programs [F10-SD7-02: verify current FY2025–2026 WIOA appropriation level and proposed reductions at DOL ETA budget materials — UV-04].

**Registered Apprenticeship, 29 U.S.C. § 50; 29 C.F.R. Part 29.** National Apprenticeship Act of 1937. DOL Office of Apprenticeship registers programs; sets standards: minimum 2,000 hours on-the-job learning; related technical instruction; progressive wage schedule; written apprenticeship standards.

*Building trades registered apprenticeships in PA-3:* IBEW Local 98 (electricians) 5-year apprenticeship — the premier wage-mobility pathway in PA-3's workforce development landscape; journeyman wages approximately $55+/hour [F10-SD7-03: verify current IBEW Local 98 journeyman wage scale at IBEW98.com]. Application process: entrance examination (math and reading); ranked waiting list; acceptance contingent on list position and construction-activity demand. Historical racial exclusion patterns: IBEW Local 98 and other mechanical trades have faced documented racial inclusion gaps; Philadelphia OEO monitors diversity outcomes at City-funded projects [F10-SD7-04: verify current IBEW Local 98 apprenticeship demographic composition from OEO or OFCCP data].

**Second Chance Act, 42 U.S.C. § 17501 et seq.** Enacted 2008; reauthorized. Grants to states, local governments, and nonprofits for reentry services including employment services, mentoring, and supportive housing. D17 boundary: D10 SD7 owns the employment-side reentry services; D17 (planned) will own the criminal-justice-system interface.

## Section 3: Pathway Tracing

**Pathway A: Workforce Development — Reentry to Building Trades.** A 28-year-old man from Strawberry Mansion released from state incarceration after a 3-year term; GED; construction day-labor experience; goal of entering the electrical trade. Visits North Philadelphia CareerLink; WIOA Adult priority-of-service designation. Career exploration identifies IBEW Local 98 apprenticeship as target pathway; pre-apprenticeship program as entry point. Pre-apprenticeship: typically 6–12 weeks; covers OSHA 10; basic math and reading reinforcement; construction trades introduction. IBEW Local 98 application involves math/reading entrance examination; ranked waiting list; individualized assessment of criminal record required under Philadelphia Ban the Box. If qualifies: waiting list extends 1–3 years depending on construction activity volume. If accepted: 5-year registered apprenticeship; journeyman electrician wages ($55+/hour) upon completion — the highest wage-mobility outcome available through any PA-3 workforce development pathway. Total timeline from CareerLink intake to journeyman wages: 7–9 years. The historical racial exclusion pattern of IBEW Local 98's apprenticeship means this pathway has not been equally accessible to Black workers from North Philadelphia even when they navigate the gatekeeping steps.

**Pathway B: WIOA ITA — Short Certification Pathway.** A West Philadelphia resident, 35 years old, long-term unemployed; prior retail work history; seeks retraining. CareerLink intake and assessment; ITA approved for a Certified Nursing Assistant (CNA) certification program at CCP (8–12 weeks; approximately $3,500 tuition — within ITA maximum). Completes CNA certification; passes Pennsylvania competency examination. Places as a CNA; starting wages $14–17/hour — genuine employment outcome. Structural ceiling: advancing to Licensed Practical Nurse (LPN) or Registered Nurse (RN) requires a 12-month LPN or 2-year ADN program at CCP; tuition $8,000–$18,000+; the ITA maximum does not cover the full credential-length program cost. WIOA's ITA structure funds the entry point; it does not fund the career ladder. The research-supported intervention (CUNY ASAP model; Scrivener et al., MDRC 2015) — intensive case management, full financial support, reduced academic burden, 2–3 year engagement — is not replicable at scale under WIOA ITA funding levels.

## Section 6: Conversational Narrative Note

SD7's central finding is the project's most persistent finding across domains: the formal provision exists, is locally well-designed by national standards, and is genuinely accessed by a large number of PA-3 residents. Philadelphia Works administers a functioning, federally compliant workforce development system. CareerLink sites exist in the sub-areas with the highest need. WorkReady serves thousands of youth. The ITA funds real certifications that lead to real jobs.

The gap is in the distance between "a real job" and "genuine wage mobility." The workforce development system is calibrated to produce job placements in part because that is what WIOA's performance metrics measure and fund. For a PA-3 resident with moderate barriers, a CNA certification is a real step forward. For a resident with significant barriers — long-term unemployment; reentry; limited literacy; no childcare; unstable housing — the 8-week CNA pathway is the system delivering the service it is funded to deliver, in a district where the evidence consistently shows that the intervention needed is 2–3 years of intensive supported engagement.

The anchor institution community-hiring story at SD7 is the employment-outcomes parallel to D8-Q2 (procurement) and D10-Q1's SD4 engagement (labor-relations). Penn is physically adjacent to West Philadelphia's highest-unemployment census tracts. Aramark employs hundreds of workers at Penn's campus. The Buy West Philadelphia commitment is a real institutional statement. Whether those facts produce employment outcomes for West Philadelphia residents at any measurable rate above what the market would produce without the commitment is the held-open question. Phase 3 retrieval did not surface primary-source institutional documentation of outcomes; D10-Q1 remains held-open per the discipline.

## Section 8: Representation Question Closure

**Element 1 — Formal provision:** The WIOA framework, implemented through Philadelphia Works and PA CareerLink Philadelphia, provides a publicly funded workforce development infrastructure that reaches tens of thousands of PA-3 residents annually. ITA funding supports real certifications for real jobs. WorkReady Philadelphia serves PA-3 youth through PYN. Registered apprenticeship pathways — particularly in the building trades — offer the highest wage-mobility outcomes available in the PA-3 workforce development landscape. Second Chance Act-funded reentry services address a documented PA-3 need. The anchor institutions are formally present in the district and have made employment commitments through CBAs and community benefit programs.

**Element 2 — Actual receipt:** What PA-3 constituents actually receive is structured by the design of the formal provision. WIOA's employer-demand-driven, ITA-funded model delivers short certifications and job placements for workers with moderate barriers. For PA-3's most disadvantaged populations — the long-term unemployed Black adults in Strawberry Mansion, the returning citizens in Kingsessing, the disconnected youth in Mill Creek — the formal provision delivers the service it is funded to deliver, which is not the intensive, sustained, credential-length intervention that research supports as effective for this population. The building trades pathway offers the strongest wage outcomes but has not been equally accessible to Black workers from North and West Philadelphia. The anchor institution community-hiring commitments have not, at the operational level, produced employment connections whose scope and enforcement are publicly documented — D10-Q1 held-open; Phase 3 confirms absence of primary-source outcome data (UV-07).

**Element 3 — Account for the gap:** The ITA-cap gap is a public investment gap: the federal funding level for WIOA is set by Congress; the ITA maximum is set by Philadelphia Works within that allocation; the allocation does not support the intensive model that evidence supports at scale. The building trades access barrier is a structural gatekeeping gap — entrance exams, waiting lists, criminal-record review, historical network-based recruitment — operating within an occupational training system largely insulated from the WIOA accountability framework. The community-hiring gap (D10-Q1) is traceable to the subcontracting structure documented in SD4: Penn cannot compel Aramark to hire from identified ZIP codes unless it contractually requires Aramark to do so and monitors compliance, which the available evidence does not confirm it does.

**Element 4 — Meaning for representation analysis:** D10-Thread B SECONDARY at SD7 completes the anchor institution analysis across the domain cycle: D7 (real estate), D8 (procurement), D9 (fiscal), D10 SD1 (governance context), D10 SD3 (workplace safety), D10 SD4 (labor relations — structural mechanism PRIMARY), D10 SD5 (federal contractor OFCCP — confirmed eliminated), D10 SD7 (community-hiring commitments). D10-Q1 held-open at magnitude level across both primary (SD4) and secondary (SD7) engagements. Congressional representation at SD7: WIOA reauthorization, ITA maximum levels, apprenticeship diversity requirements (29 C.F.R. Part 30), and Second Chance Act reauthorization are all within federal legislative scope.
