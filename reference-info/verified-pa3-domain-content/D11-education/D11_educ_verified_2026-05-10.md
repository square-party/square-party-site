## Synthesis Section 1: What This Domain Established

D11 establishes the K-12-through-postsecondary educational architecture governing PA-3 constituents and traces, across seven sub-domains, where federal floor, PA state-primary substance, and Philadelphia local administration interact to produce educational-trajectory outcomes that correlate with the district's documented racial geography and wealth-gap patterns. The findings are organized below by sub-domain, with cross-cutting findings consolidated at the end. Evidence-type labels follow Standard 5: [D] directly documented; [SD] structurally derived; [SI] structural inference.

**1. K-12 substantive architecture is structurally underfunded by constitutional measure, with three within-domain analytical-tension mechanisms concentrated in a single SD (SD1).** The William Penn ruling (February 7, 2023; final July 21, 2023) established that PA's school funding system is unconstitutionally inadequate — the first PA holding that education is a fundamental right under the state constitution; the Commonwealth Court documented an adequacy gap of approximately $4.6–$6.2 billion (G11-SD1-02) [D]. Approximately $1 billion in remedy-driven adequacy investment across FY2024-25 and FY2025-26 leaves a remaining gap of approximately $3.8 billion. *Verification update (2026-05-10): Governor Shapiro's FY2026-27 budget proposal (February 3, 2026), confirmed from PA.gov primary source, directs an additional $565 million through the bipartisan adequacy formula. Pennsylvania Policy Center analysis (February 2026) estimates the remaining adequacy gap at approximately $3.3 billion if the Shapiro proposal is enacted. As of May 10, 2026, the FY2026-27 budget has not been enacted (June 30, 2026 deadline). Enacted state therefore remains at approximately $3.8B remaining gap pending FY2026-27 legislative action. See TC-01 (temporal-changes sidecar) for vector tracking. See MC-05.* Federal funding to SDP declined from approximately $550.8 million (FY2024, ESSER-supplemented) to approximately $16 million in the FY2026 operating budget (G11-SD1-01) [D for the structural ESSER cliff; F-flagged for Trump-era specific reduction figures]. SDP is the only school district in Pennsylvania without authority to levy its own taxes (G11-SD1-03) [D]. Three within-domain analytical-tension MCs operate simultaneously: MC49 (charter per-pupil funding formula — substantive school choice AND structural district-resource displacement; G11-SD1-05) [D for stranded-cost dynamic at 44–68% recovery; F-flagged for SDP-specific aggregate annual charter tuition total]; MC50 (PA school funding formula — substantive equity remedy AND structural disparity reproduction through local-property-tax-heavy architecture; G11-SD1-06) [D for William Penn ruling; trajectory MEDIUM]; MC51 (EITC PA / OSTC scholarship tax credits — substantive school-choice support AND state-revenue diversion to private/religious schools; G11-SD1-07) [D for tax-credit architecture; SI for $340M scale pending F11-SD1-02]. The concentration of three within-domain analytical-tensions in a single SD is project-novel.

**2. Postsecondary substantive architecture provides formal HEA Title IV access that produces a structural debt-without-credential gap concentrated at PA-3-serving institutions, with state-related institution fiscal instability as a parallel access risk.** HEA Title IV provides Pell + Direct Loan + PHEAA architecture that reaches CCP's 60% Pell enrollment and Temple's high-Pell undergraduate population (G11-SD2-02 substantive side) [D]. The structural gap operates at completion: cohort default rates and gainful-employment outcomes at community colleges and lower-selectivity institutions are structurally elevated relative to selective research universities because student economic vulnerability is higher and completion rates are lower (G11-SD2-02 structural side) [SI pending F11-SD2-06 for SDP-feeder-population-specific figures]. FAFSA completion is itself a structural access barrier preceding enrollment for SDP graduates (G11-SD2-01) [SI; F11-SD2-05]. Temple University's enrollment decline of approximately 10,000 students since 2017, $200 million revenue loss, and $60 million fiscal deficit (G11-SD2-03) [D] is an educational-access risk for the North Philadelphia constituency that cannot access Penn (selectivity) or afford Drexel (cost). Anchor-engaged community-engagement educational programs at Penn / Temple / Drexel / Jefferson (G11-SD2-04) operate as a Both/And: substantive programming exists; scale relative to community need is not established by SD2 evidence and is convertible to commitment-vs-outcome HOM at Phase 3 if discrete-mechanism + quantified-commitments + measurable-outcomes territory surfaces. Post-SFFA enrollment composition has shifted within PA-3 anchor institutions: Penn's Black undergraduate enrollment declined (from 9.4% to 8.6%) while Temple's rose substantially (from 20.9% to 29.7%) and Drexel and St. Joseph's saw underrepresented-minority increases (G11-SD2-05) [D for enrollment figures; MEDIUM confidence on redistribution interpretation], shifting the federal representation access-advocacy emphasis toward CCP and Temple capacity rather than selective-institution admissions policy.

**3. Early childhood educational architecture has no constitutional or statutory entitlement floor; coverage is appropriation-determined and structurally insufficient.** PHLpreK's 5,250 seats plus Head Start and PA Pre-K Counts combined do not serve all income-eligible three-and-four-year-olds in Philadelphia (G11-SD3-01) [D for PHLpreK count; SI for shortfall magnitude pending F11-SD3-03]. PHLpreK depends structurally on Philadelphia Beverage Tax receipts — a consumption-tax revenue stream with no constitutional protection or statutory entitlement floor (G11-SD3-02) [D]. Part-day program structure interacts with full-time-employment family circumstances to produce a navigation-burden access barrier (G11-SD3-03) [D]. The early childhood educator staffing crisis independently constrains program capacity (G11-SD3-04) [D for national pattern; SI for PA-3 magnitude]. Federal House representation has Head Start appropriation as the most direct early-childhood lever; PHLpreK and PA Pre-K Counts are state and local instruments outside direct federal control.

**4. Specialized populations and civil-rights architecture provides comprehensive formal entitlement; structural gaps operate at identification, definition, and enforcement-posture vulnerability.** IDEA produces racially patterned identification outcomes — over-identification of Black students in the high-restriction "emotional disturbance" category and under-identification in "autism" and "specific learning disability" relative to white peers (G11-SD4-01) [D for national CRDC pattern; SI for SDP magnitude pending F11-SD4-06]. The MC19 McKinney-Vento Title I / Title VII definitional gap (carry-forward from D7 SD6 G7-SD6-03) means doubled-up families are Title VII-eligible for educational services but Title I-excluded for CoC housing services; PIT counts and HMIS systematically undercount housing-instability prevalence relevant to the education system (G11-SD4-02) [D for definitional gap; SI for magnitude]. SDP's Portuguese-speaking ELL population has surpassed Mandarin as the second-largest non-English home language while bilingual programming infrastructure remains Spanish-dominant (G11-SD4-03) [D for enrollment growth; SI for staffing-gap magnitude]. ED OCR enforcement of Title VI / Title IX / Section 504 / McKinney-Vento protections is administratively variable across administrations (G11-SD4-04) [D for documented Trump-era posture changes; Standard 11 administrative vulnerability]. Section 504's request-driven K-12 architecture produces an access disparity relative to IDEA's affirmative child-find obligation for families lacking advocacy capacity (G11-SD4-05) [D].

**5. Adult basic education capacity is structurally below adult literacy need; federal House representation has direct WIOA Title II appropriation leverage.** WIOA Title II program capacity nationally serves a fraction of the adult population with literacy needs (approximately 10:1 need-to-capacity ratio nationally); Philadelphia-specific gap is F-flagged at SD5 (G11-SD5-01) [SI for Philadelphia magnitude; D for national structural pattern]. Integrated Education and Training (IET) supply does not meet demand for simultaneous literacy + workforce credential progress (G11-SD5-02) [SI; F-flagged]. Adult ELA programming is more developed for Spanish-dominant learners than for Haitian Creole, Arabic, Portuguese, and African-language adult learners — a parallel to the SD4 K-12 ELL language-mix pattern (G11-SD5-03) [SI].

**6. Career and technical education's primary structural gap operates at the CTE-to-apprenticeship interface, not within the CTE program architecture itself.** Building-trades registered apprenticeship programs in Philadelphia have documented underrepresentation of Black workers relative to their share of the Philadelphia labor market (D10 SD7 primary; F11-SD6-03 for PA-3-specific demographic data). SDP CTE completion in building-trades programs is not a direct pipeline to apprenticeship entry; the union apprenticeship application process is a separate gatekeeping step (G11-SD6-01) [SI from D10 SD7 patterns; PA-3-specific magnitude F-flagged]. Statewide articulation agreements between secondary CTE and CCP exist; operational translation to enrollment and credit transfer is incomplete because of navigation barriers (G11-SD6-02) [SI from national pattern; PA-3-specific F-flagged]. Federal Perkins V appropriation and DOL apprenticeship non-discrimination enforcement are the primary federal levers.

**7. Operational integration is the locus of D11's emergent-from-interaction HOM (school-to-prison pipeline) and of the funding-obligation mismatch pattern at AHERA compliance and school-based mental health.** MC48 (school-to-prison pipeline) is held maximally open as the project's first primary engagement with the emergent-from-interaction HOM shape (G11-SD7-01) [D for each component mechanism; SI for emergent magnitude]. The mechanism inventory: school discipline architecture; civil-rights differential application (Title VI / Section 504 / IDEA disparities); truancy enforcement; SRO presence + mandatory referral provisions; criminal-justice involvement. No mechanism is identified as primary; pipeline magnitude is held open through all four phases. The substantive school-safety architecture (PBIS; Office of School Safety; SDP-PPD MOU; threat-assessment protocols) operates as Both/And: real educational-environment safety benefits AND structural over-discipline of Black and Latino students (G11-SD7-02) [D]. School-as-eligibility-gate operational integration depends on staff capacity constrained by the same SD1 adequacy-gap fiscal architecture (G11-SD7-03) [SI for system-level pattern; SDP-specific scale F-flagged]. AHERA federal-criminal-floor compliance obligations interact with William Penn-diagnosed funding inadequacy to produce a structural funding-obligation mismatch (G11-SD7-04) [D]. The ESSER expiration eliminated supplemental school-based mental health funding (G11-SD7-05) [D for cliff; SI for SDP-specific magnitude pending F11-SD7-07]; SDP base operating budget cannot absorb ESSER-level staffing without separate replacement funding.

**Cross-cutting Finding A: Two HOM shapes are operative in D11 — the project's first domain to carry both shapes simultaneously.** Commitment-vs-outcome HOM (G11-SD1-04: Penn Alexander catchment displacement magnitude relative to other West Philadelphia displacement mechanisms) is the fifth confirmed instance of this shape project-wide (G7-SD1-03 + D8-Q2 + D10-Q1 + D24-Q1 + G11-SD1-04). Emergent-from-interaction HOM (G11-SD7-01: school-to-prison pipeline) is the project's first primary engagement with this distinct HOM shape. The two shapes share the discipline of holding magnitude open absent ranking evidence; they differ in what is held open. Commitment-vs-outcome HOM holds open the contribution magnitude of an anchor's specific mechanism (catchment premium) relative to other documented mechanisms producing the same outcome category (displacement). Emergent-from-interaction HOM holds open the magnitude of a system-level outcome (pipeline) that no single architecture explicitly produces and that arises from the simultaneous operation of multiple formally functional architectures on the same constituent population. This is project-novel territory and is candidate methodology absorption for M2 v1.3.

**Cross-cutting Finding B: D11 occupies the structural-reproduction node in the project's cumulative racial wealth-disadvantage chain.** D11 inherits the chain explicitly: D2 (health → poverty interaction) → D3/D5 (mental health and disability) → D7 (residential segregation; mortgage denial-rate gaps) → D8 (commercial credit access; SBDC pipeline) → D10 (labor-market exclusion; building-trades demographics) → D11 (educational architecture reproduces the same racial geography in the next generation). The same wealth-disadvantage geography that produces residential mortgage denial-rate gaps in D7, commercial credit-access gaps in D8, and labor-market exclusion in D10 is reproduced in part through the educational architecture D11 documents — primarily through the local-property-tax-heavy state funding architecture (G11-SD1-06; MC50), the charter per-pupil funding formula (G11-SD1-05; MC49), and the early-childhood appropriation-determined coverage gap (G11-SD3-01) that delays disadvantage compounding from K-entry forward.

**Cross-cutting Finding C: Substantive-program-vs-structural-impact is a cross-mechanism pattern of D11 K-12 architecture, not a feature of any single mechanism.** Three substantive K-12 programs each operate as designed while simultaneously producing structural impact contributing to the inequity each program addresses: charter expansion serves families AND displaces SDP resources (G11-SD1-05; MC49); the school funding formula post-William-Penn investments serve adequacy AND the local-tax-base architecture continues to reproduce disparity (G11-SD1-06; MC50); EITC PA / OSTC scholarship tax credits provide school-choice support AND divert state revenue from the public-school adequacy remedy (G11-SD1-07; MC51). The pattern is not produced by any single program's design failure — each program operates as its statute prescribes. The pattern is produced by the simultaneous operation of formally functional programs on the same constituent population in the same fiscal envelope. This is the cross-mechanism finding the substructure §2A foregrounded for synthesis-level naming.

**Cross-cutting Finding D: The anchor-institution multi-role frame is extended to a sixth dimension at D11.** Penn / Temple / Drexel / Jefferson / CHOP have been treated across the project's prior domains as fiscal-authority constituent (D9), commerce-and-industry actor (D8), employer (D10), and real-estate actor (D7). The six-dimension anchor framework is now fully operational; D25 (research enterprise) is the seventh dimension when that domain cycles.

## Section 2: Authority Chain

### Constitutional Foundation

Federal K-12 education authority rests on the **Spending Clause** (U.S. Const. Art. I § 8 cl. 1): Congress conditions federal education grants on state and local compliance with federal requirements (ESSA, IDEA, McKinney-Vento, Title IX). The Spending Clause does not authorize federal prescription of educational content or structure — it establishes the conditional-grant architecture that makes federal statutes operational. The **14th Amendment** (equal protection; due process) grounds the anti-discrimination architecture (Title VI, Title IX, Section 504) and provides the constitutional basis for judicial challenges to unequal educational conditions. The federal government has no direct constitutional authority over K-12 education; federal power is exercised through conditional grants and civil rights enforcement.

Pennsylvania education authority rests on **PA Const. Art. III § 14** (the Education Clause), which directs the General Assembly to provide for a "thorough and efficient system of public education." *William Penn School District v. Pennsylvania Department of Education et al.*, Commonwealth Court (Judge Renée Cohn Jubelirer, February 7, 2023; final July 21, 2023), held that Pennsylvania's school funding system is unconstitutional under both the Education Clause and equal protection grounds — the first Pennsylvania holding that education is a fundamental right under the state constitution [Source 3]. The ruling declared the local-property-tax-reliance architecture constitutionally inadequate: tying educational resources to local wealth produces structural underfunding for districts with lower local property tax base, including SDP. The adequacy gap documented at trial: approximately $4.6–$6.2 billion [Source 3]. This constitutional ruling is the load-bearing legal context for the PA state funding formula analysis throughout SD1.

**PA Const. Art. IX** (property taxation authority) and the **Homestead Exemption** provisions govern the property tax levy through which SDP receives the local share of its operating budget. The School District of Philadelphia is the only school district in Pennsylvania without authority to levy its own taxes — SDP relies on city and state sources for 99% of its operating budget [Source 2]. This structural anomaly (rooted in the 1965 Philadelphia Home Rule Charter and the Pennsylvania Intergovernmental Cooperation Authority framework) is a persistent administrative-vulnerability feature of SDP's fiscal architecture (Standard 11; addressed in §7 gap analysis).

### Federal Architecture

**Title I, Part A of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 1965 as amended by ESSA**, 20 U.S.C. § 6301 et seq. (P.L. 114-95, December 10, 2015). The principal federal grant program for K-12 public education; allocates funds to states by formula based on the number of low-income children; states sub-allocate to LEAs (school districts and charter LEAs); LEAs target resources to high-poverty schools. SDP receives among the largest Title I allocations of any single district in the country given its concentration of low-income students. *Statutory stability:* HIGH at the statutory level. *Appropriation stability:* VARIABLE — ESSER pandemic-relief supplemental funding expired in September 2024 after a multi-year wind-down [Source 2], and the Trump administration has reduced Title I, Title II, and Title III regular program allocations [F11-SD1-01; see §9].

**Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA)**, 20 U.S.C. § 1400 et seq. The federal grant-and-entitlement statute governing special education. IDEA Part B provides formula grants to states (flow-through to LEAs) conditioned on provision of a free appropriate public education (FAPE) in the least restrictive environment (LRE) to all children with disabilities aged 3–21. IDEA Part C governs early intervention (birth–2) and is SD3 territory. Implementing regulations at 34 C.F.R. Parts 300 (Part B) and 303 (Part C). PA's implementing regulations at 22 Pa. Code Ch. 14 (special education; the "Chapter 14 regs"). *Statutory stability:* HIGH. IDEA's entitlement architecture is durable federal law; the racial-disparity pattern in IDEA identification and placement is an ongoing enforcement concern (cross-reference D3 SD4: G1-SD4-06 IDEA-ED placement framework + racial disparity; SD4 is primary engagement for special education at D11).

**Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972**, 20 U.S.C. § 1681. Prohibits sex discrimination in any education program receiving federal financial assistance. Implemented by ED Office for Civil Rights. Title IX is SD4 territory for its civil-rights enforcement architecture; SD1 notes the statutory hook as part of the federal-floor authority chain governing SDP and charter LEAs as federal financial assistance recipients.

**Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973**, 29 U.S.C. § 794. Prohibits disability discrimination in any program receiving federal financial assistance. In K-12 education, Section 504 operates alongside IDEA: students who are not IDEA-eligible may still qualify for Section 504 accommodations. Section 504 is SD4 territory for substantive analysis; SD1 notes it as part of the federal civil-rights overlay on SDP and charter LEAs.

**McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act, Title VII — Education for Homeless Children and Youth**, 42 U.S.C. § 11431 et seq. Requires states and LEAs to ensure homeless children have immediate enrollment in school and access to educational services. The Title I / Title VII definitional-gap finding (MC19) is SD4 primary territory per substructure decomposition (see D7 SD6 G7-SD6-03 and the cross-reference protocol established at the D7 cycle); SD1 notes McKinney-Vento as a federal floor authority for the institutional-set obligation.

**Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA)**, 20 U.S.C. § 1232g. Governs student education records — access, disclosure, consent architecture. FERPA is SD7 operational-integration territory (school FERPA data architecture; commercial ed-tech platforms; SDP Office of Research and Evaluation data-sharing agreements); SD1 notes it as a federal statutory floor applicable to SDP and charter LEAs.

**ESSA Title IV, Part A — Student Support and Academic Enrichment (SSAE)**, 20 U.S.C. § 7111. Formula grants to states and LEAs for well-rounded education, safe/healthy learning environments, and effective technology use. Part of the ESSA consolidated grant architecture through which SDP and PA charter LEAs draw federal program funds.

### State Architecture

**PA School Code of 1949**, 24 P.S. § 1-101 et seq. The comprehensive Pennsylvania statute governing public K-12 education. The PA School Code is the dominant substantive authority governing SDP: it specifies Board of Education governance, superintendent authority, teacher certification, mandatory attendance (24 P.S. § 13-1326), curriculum requirements, special education supplementary to IDEA, transportation obligations, and the structure of the state funding formula applicable to SDP and other LEAs. *Statutory stability:* HIGH as a structural matter; specific funding formula provisions have been the subject of the William Penn litigation and post-2023 legislative response.

**PA State Funding Formula — Post-William Penn Architecture.** The PA school funding formula distributes state Basic Education Funding (BEF) to districts based on a weighted-ADM (average daily membership) formula that accounts for the number of students, their characteristics (English Language Learners, poverty, concentrated poverty), and the district's local fiscal capacity (equalized mills). The formula has been the subject of sustained adequacy litigation culminating in the William Penn ruling [Source 3].

*Post-William Penn legislative response:* The Pennsylvania General Assembly has responded through incremental appropriation increases in two budget cycles:
- FY2024-25 PA budget: $1.1 billion increase for K-12 (largest single-year increase in state history); $232 million more for Philadelphia schools; $100 million cyber charter reimbursement fund [Source 9].
- FY2025-26 PA budget (enacted November 12, 2025 — 135 days late; longest budget impasse in state history): $565 million in adequacy-related school funding ($526.4 million adequacy formula; $32.2 million tax equity supplement; $6.4 million minimum-baseline provision for ~500 districts) [Source 3].
- Cumulative investment across FY2024-25 and FY2025-26: approximately $1 billion toward the adequacy gap.
- Remaining adequacy gap: approximately $3.8 billion [Source 3].
- FY2026-27 budget (Governor Shapiro proposal, February 19, 2026): $151 million more for Philadelphia; additional $565 million statewide for adequacy; cyber charter write-off expansion; estimated $75 million in additional district savings [Source 10]. As of May 2026, the FY2026-27 budget has not been enacted; deadline June 30, 2026.

PA relies on local property taxes to fund schools more heavily than all but six other states, with only approximately 38% of school funding coming from the state level [Source 3]. This structural feature is what the William Penn ruling identified as the constitutional violation — and what makes SDP's structural position particularly acute given that (a) SDP cannot levy its own taxes and (b) SDP's property tax base reflects the documented wealth-gap that makes heavy local-property-tax reliance structurally inequitable.

**PA Charter School Law**, 24 P.S. § 17-1701-A et seq. (enacted 1997, amended multiple times). Authorizes the establishment and operation of charter schools as independently operated public schools. Charter LEAs must: enroll all students in the district; accept students with disabilities and ELL students; follow applicable federal statutes; not charge tuition. Charter LEAs are authorized by the local school board (or, for cyber charters, by PDE directly for statewide charters). The funding formula for charter LEAs is governed by Section 1725-A: SDP must pay a per-pupil tuition rate (PDE Form 363 rate) based on SDP's budgeted total expenditures per ADM for each resident student enrolled in a charter LEA. The per-pupil rate has two tiers: non-special-education rate and special-education rate (calculated as a percentage of SDP's aggregate special-education spending). This funding formula is the statutory mechanism that produces the MC49 Both/And dynamic analyzed in §6 and §7.

**PA Special Education Regulations**, 22 Pa. Code Ch. 14. Pennsylvania's implementing regulations for IDEA Part B. The Chapter 14 regs govern IEP development, placement decisions, procedural safeguards, and the delivery of special education services within PA's state educational system. They supplement and, in some respects, exceed the IDEA federal floor. Special education is SD4 primary territory; SD1 notes Chapter 14 as part of the state-primary authority chain.

**PA EITC PA (Educational Improvement Tax Credit) and OSTC (Opportunity Scholarship Tax Credit)**. EITC PA (72 P.S. § 8701-C et seq.) provides corporate tax credits for donations to Educational Improvement Organizations (EIOs) and Scholarship Organizations (SOs). OSTC (72 P.S. § 8701-G et seq.) provides tax credits for donations to scholarship organizations serving students in "low-achieving" schools. In 2022-23, combined EITC PA + OSTC provided approximately $340 million in tax credits [F11-SD1-02; Source 18]. *Verification update (2026-05-10): PA FY2024-25 budget raised combined EITC/OSTC statutory caps to $525 million annually (EITC +$50M, OSTC +$5M increase per Education Voters PA FY2024-25 budget analysis, July 2024). The $340M figure represents 2022-23 program amounts; the caps have grown substantially. Actual amounts claimed in 2024-25 require PA Department of Revenue annual tax-credit report (not retrieved within three-search budget; see UV-01 in unverified-items sidecar). Structural-direction finding confirmed: the revenue-diversion mechanism operates at growing scale. D9 verified file remains primary engagement for the authoritative fiscal-year claimed amounts. See MC-08.* These programs divert state corporate tax revenue to private and religious school scholarship funds; the fiscal-architecture analysis is D9 primary (per substructure §5 cross-reference), but MC51 (Both/And designation) gives SD1 the analytical hook for the cost-and-contribution presentation. *T11-SD1-01: EITC PA/OSTC cap expansion trajectory — PA legislature has incrementally raised program caps; 2024-25 cap levels warrant T-flag for current-figure update at Phase 3.*

## Section 3: Pathway Tracing

**The PA-3 K-12 Pathway: Catchment Assignment, Charter Choice, and the Funding Architecture in Between**

The representative K-12 pathway for a PA-3 constituent begins at school assignment — a decision that operates through the catchment system for SDP district schools, the charter lottery for charter LEAs, and private-choice mechanisms for nonpublic schools. The pathway is not a single-agency claim process; it is a multi-system selection and resource-allocation problem that operates at the intersection of residential geography, institutional availability, and fiscal architecture.

*Triggering condition:* A PA-3 family with a school-age child approaches K-12 enrollment.

*First-stage decision: Catchment vs. choice.* SDP operates primarily through a catchment (neighborhood) assignment system: each residential address falls within the catchment zone of a specific SDP elementary, middle, and high school. The quality of the catchment school — measured by School Progress Report score, test proficiency rates, available programming, building condition, teacher experience — varies substantially across PA-3's four sub-areas. A family in the catchment zone of a high-performing criteria-based school faces a different first-stage condition than a family in the catchment zone of a chronically under-resourced neighborhood school. The charter lottery introduces a parallel choice pathway: PA-3 families may apply to charter LEAs whose seats are distributed by lottery (with limited geographic, sibling, and founder preferences per Section 1725-A). Charter seats are finite; lottery outcomes are probabilistic; waitlists are common at high-demand charter LEAs (Mastery; KIPP).

*Penn Alexander as the representative anchor-catchment interaction.* Penn Alexander School (K-8; West Philadelphia catchment; Penn-affiliated university-community partnership school) is the project's load-bearing example of the catchment premium — where an institutional partnership has produced a school whose reputation and outcomes generate property-value effects in adjacent catchment zones that contribute to residential displacement for lower-income families. The Penn Alexander catchment premium is confirmed in D7 SD1 (Profile A — longtime Black homeowner in West Philadelphia Core facing Penn-adjacent acquisition pressure; Penn Alexander school catchment as engaged-community anchor with adjacent-property-value-increase consequence) [Source 11]. The SD1 pathway traces the mechanism: Penn institutional partnership (funding; teacher placement; enrichment programming) → catchment-school quality enhancement → catchment-zone property-value increase → housing-cost pressure → displacement of lower-income families whose children the school's educational environment was designed to serve. The magnitude of Penn Alexander's contribution to West Philadelphia displacement relative to other mechanisms (Penn real-estate acquisition; Schuylkill Yards; broader West Philadelphia gentrification corridor) is held open per HOM discipline: G11-SD1-04 [see §7; §9].

*Funding-allocation consequence.* Once a student enrolls — whether in an SDP catchment school, a charter LEA, or a nonpublic school through scholarship — the funding architecture determines what per-pupil resources follow. For SDP catchment students: the school's resource allocation depends on SDP's global budget, which is determined by (a) city property tax millage share; (b) state Basic Education Funding formula distribution; (c) federal Title I and IDEA formula allocations. For charter LEA students: SDP is required to pay the PDE Form 363 per-pupil rate to the charter LEA; this payment comes from SDP's overall budget, constituting a direct transfer that creates the MC49 resource-displacement dynamic. For nonpublic school students: EITC PA/OSTC scholarship tax credits reduce state corporate tax revenue that would otherwise flow toward the general fund (and potentially toward school funding); the MC51 revenue-diversion dynamic operates here.

*The stranded-cost problem.* When a student moves from an SDP school to a charter LEA, SDP must pay the per-pupil charter tuition rate but cannot proportionately reduce its operating costs. Teachers, building maintenance, utilities, and transportation costs associated with the school the student left do not immediately decrease when one student departs. Research for Action's analysis of six Pennsylvania school districts found that even five years after a student's departure for a charter, districts recovered only 44–68% of the costs associated with the charter tuition payment — a persistent stranded-cost effect [Source 6]. For SDP, which services approximately 63,964 brick-and-mortar charter students (32% of total Philadelphia public school enrollment) plus 14,252 cyber charter students, the aggregate stranded-cost burden is a structural feature of SDP's fiscal architecture, not an exceptional event [Sources 1, 6].

*McKinney-Vento pathway (architecture-level note).* Students experiencing housing instability in PA-3 — the McKinney-Vento-eligible population — face the catchment-assignment problem with additional severity: residential mobility may cross catchment boundaries frequently; FERPA records-transfer obligations may lag enrollment; SDP's McKinney-Vento homeless liaison is responsible for immediate enrollment and transportation provision. The substantive McKinney-Vento pathway is SD4 territory (and engages D7 SD6 G7-SD6-03 cross-reference for the Title I/Title VII definitional-gap finding); SD1 notes the institutional-set obligation.

## Section 6: Conversational Narrative Note

The three within-domain analytical-tensions concentrated in SD1 — charter expansion (MC49), school funding formula (MC50), and EITC PA/OSTC scholarship tax credits (MC51) — share a structural feature that SD1 is the first sub-domain in the project to hold simultaneously in three instances: a substantive program does what it claims, and its structural mechanics contribute to the inequity the program addresses.

Charter schools in Philadelphia provide real educational alternatives for families navigating a district whose catchment schools are uneven in quality. Families who access a high-performing charter LEA and whose children benefit from that enrollment are not experiencing a fiction — the substantive program delivers. But the funding formula that makes charter schools possible in Pennsylvania prices each student's departure from SDP at SDP's full per-pupil rate, generating a sustained per-unit fiscal loss for SDP that constrains improvement of the catchment schools that serve everyone who didn't win the lottery. The charter expansion simultaneously serves and strains the public school system it exists within.

The school funding formula (MC50) is more directly a structural-disparity mechanism: the post-William Penn adequacy formula is intended to reduce the structural underfunding gap diagnosed by the Commonwealth Court, and it produces measurable improvements for SDP and for Philadelphia charter LEAs (charters receive an additional ~$650 per pupil when SDP's spending rises, because charter tuition is pegged to SDP per-pupil spending) [Source 8]. The substantive adequacy effort is real. But the formula's architectural dependence on local property tax wealth means that as long as the ~$3.8 billion adequacy gap persists, the districts and schools most affected by local-wealth-deprivation are the ones farthest from constitutional adequacy — a structural-disparity-reproduction dynamic that the formula's adequacy investment is trying to correct faster than the underlying local-wealth differential widens.

EITC PA/OSTC (MC51) is the most discrete-mechanism of the three: state corporate tax credits fund private school scholarships, reducing state revenue that could otherwise flow to public school adequacy. The tax credit programs are designed to expand school-choice options for low-income students in low-achieving schools; many families who use OSTC scholarships have genuine alternatives to chronically under-resourced public schools. The revenue-diversion structural concern is not that individual families make bad choices — it is that the tax-credit architecture creates a state revenue stream flowing to private (including religious) schools without the accountability infrastructure applicable to charter schools, while reducing the revenue available for the constitutional adequacy remedy the public system requires.

D11-Thread C names this cross-mechanism pattern for Phase 2 synthesis consolidation. SD1 holds all three tensions open; the synthesis file will ask whether the pattern itself — substantive-program-as-structural-impact-contributor — is analytically load-bearing for the project's overall representation framework.

## Section 8: Representation Question Closure

**Element 1 — Formal provision.** K-12 education in PA-3 is formally provided through a multi-layer architecture. At the federal floor: Title I supplemental funding for high-poverty schools; IDEA free-appropriate-public-education entitlement for students with disabilities; Title IX sex-discrimination prohibition; ESSA accountability framework; McKinney-Vento homeless education entitlements. At the state primary layer: PA School Code-mandated universal public education from kindergarten through grade 12; PA Special Ed regulations FAPE provision supplementing IDEA; post-William Penn adequacy appropriation working toward the constitutionally mandated "thorough and efficient system." At the local layer: SDP catchment school assignment for every Philadelphia resident child; charter LEA alternatives available through lottery; nonpublic school alternatives available through EITC PA/OSTC scholarships and private tuition. The formal provision is comprehensive; every PA-3 child has a legal right to a K-12 education.

**Element 2 — Actual receipt.** Actual receipt of K-12 education in PA-3 is structurally uneven in ways that correlate with the district's documented racial geography and wealth-gap patterns. Children in SDP catchment schools that are constitutionally underfunded (G11-SD1-02) receive a formal education that falls short of the "thorough and efficient" constitutional standard the Commonwealth Court identified. Children in high-demand charter LEAs experience the formal benefit of school choice but represent a selection effect from the charter lottery. Children in Penn Alexander's catchment zone receive an enriched educational environment partly funded through Penn's institutional partnership — but that catchment premium contributes to displacement pressure on lower-income families in the adjacent residential geography (G11-SD1-04; held open at magnitude). The graduation rate of 77.5% (Class of 2024) for SDP students [Source 4] — while improved — means that approximately 22.5% of SDP district students do not complete a four-year diploma on schedule; that rate is not disaggregated by sub-area or catchment-school-quality tier in this SD [F11-SD1-05].

**Element 3 — Accounting for the gap.** The gap between formal provision and actual receipt is structural, not administrative. Three mechanisms:

(a) *Adequacy gap (G11-SD1-02):* PA's school funding formula has been constitutionally diagnosed as structurally underfunding districts with lower local property tax bases; SDP operates under this structural constraint with the additional anomaly of no direct tax-levy authority (G11-SD1-03). The gap is not a program administration failure — it is a constitutional structural-design finding.

(b) *Funding formula displacement (G11-SD1-05; MC49):* The charter per-pupil payment architecture transfers resources from SDP's operating budget to charter LEAs without full cost-recovery, constraining SDP's ability to improve the catchment-school conditions that drive charter demand in the first place. The structural mechanism is legally mandated by Section 1725-A; the displacement effect is a predictable consequence of the funding formula design.

(c) *Revenue diversion (G11-SD1-07; MC51):* EITC PA/OSTC tax credits reduce state revenue available for public school adequacy investment; the constitutional adequacy remedy requires sustained state investment that is in partial competition with the tax-credit architecture for the same state corporate tax revenue.

**Element 4 — Meaning for representation.** D11-Thread A captures the representation-action structure: federal House representation has direct advocacy leverage at the federal floor (Title I appropriation; ESSER replacement; IDEA funding; ESSA oversight), but the dominant structural gaps (G11-SD1-02; G11-SD1-03; G11-SD1-05) are state-architecture problems that federal House representation can pressure but not directly correct. The representation question for SD1 is therefore whether federal advocacy — appropriation levels; accountability-condition pressure; PILOET engagement (D9); federal-program enforcement — can move the levers available at the federal floor in ways that supplement, accelerate, or condition the state-level constitutional remedy that the William Penn ruling demands. The five confirmed HOM instances project-wide (including G11-SD1-04) and the three within-domain analytical-tension MCs at SD1 collectively suggest that the K-12 architecture's structural gaps are magnitude-uncertain in ways that make single-intervention advocacy insufficient — multiple mechanisms operating simultaneously require multi-lever federal engagement.

## Section 2: Authority Chain

### Constitutional and Statutory Foundation

**Spending Clause** (U.S. Const. Art. I § 8 cl. 1) is the constitutional basis for HEA Title IV conditional grants — institutions receive federal financial aid eligibility in exchange for compliance with federal requirements (accreditation; anti-discrimination; financial responsibility; consumer protection). **14th Amendment** (equal protection) grounds Title IX and Section 504 postsecondary application; *SFFA v. Harvard* and *SFFA v. UNC* (consolidated, 600 U.S. 181, June 2023) held that race-conscious admissions programs at Harvard and UNC violated the Equal Protection Clause — ruling applicable to all institutions receiving federal financial assistance. Statutory stability of SFFA's constitutional holding: HIGH; implementation is ongoing across PA-3 anchor institutions, with documented enrollment shifts in the first post-SFFA admissions cycle [Source 21].

### Federal Architecture

**Higher Education Act of 1965 as amended**, 20 U.S.C. § 1001 et seq. The principal federal statute governing postsecondary institutions. Title IV (20 U.S.C. § 1070 et seq.) governs student financial assistance:

- **Pell Grant** (20 U.S.C. § 1070a): Need-based formula grant to low-income undergraduate students; does not require repayment; maximum award for 2024-25 was $7,395 [F11-SD2-01: verify current Pell maximum against Federal Student Aid Data Center for 2025-26 award year]. Pell functions as the baseline-access mechanism: at CCP, approximately 60% of students receive Pell [Source 20]; at Temple, Pell recipients constitute a significant share of the undergraduate population given Temple's urban-serving mission and relatively high Pell-eligible enrollment. At Penn (Ivy League), Pell recipient share is substantially lower by institutional demographics — structural selectivity gap is the SD2 gap-analysis topic.
- **William D. Ford Federal Direct Loan Program** (20 U.S.C. § 1087a et seq.): Federal direct loans to students (subsidized and unsubsidized) and parents (PLUS) and graduate students (GradPLUS). Direct Loan is the primary non-grant federal financial aid mechanism; loan-dependent access creates structural debt exposure correlated with institutional type (higher debt at for-profit and lower-completion-rate institutions; cohort-default-rate (CDR) as HEA accountability metric per 20 U.S.C. § 1085(m)).
- **Title IX** (20 U.S.C. § 1681): Applies to postsecondary institutions receiving federal financial assistance. Prohibits sex discrimination in educational programs; at postsecondary institutions includes sexual harassment, assault, athletics equity (see SD4 for K-12 Title IX treatment). Title IX at SD2 is anchor-institution compliance territory.
- **Section 504** (29 U.S.C. § 794): Applies to postsecondary institutions; requires reasonable accommodations for students with disabilities. Unlike K-12 IDEA (individual entitlement; school-district obligation), postsecondary Section 504 places the burden on the student to self-identify and request accommodation through the institution's disability services office.
- **Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA)** system: Statutory basis at 20 U.S.C. § 1090; administers the Student Aid Index (SAI, formerly Expected Family Contribution) calculation. The FAFSA simplification reforms enacted in the FAFSA Simplification Act (P.L. 116-260, 2020) became operational for the 2024-25 award year. *T11-SD2-01: FAFSA simplification implementation turbulence — the 2024-25 FAFSA rollout experienced significant delays and data-processing errors affecting the timeline of aid packages for students; the 2025-26 rollout moved to the GrantUS platform at PHEAA; ongoing implementation trajectory is temporally variable.*
- **GI Bill / Post-9/11 GI Bill / Yellow Ribbon Program** (38 U.S.C. Chapter 33): Cross-reference D24 SD3 for institutional-side GI Bill compliance architecture at PA-3 anchor institutions. SD2 notes the Yellow Ribbon Program as an institutional-side commitment mechanism (supplementary tuition funding beyond the GI Bill cap at private institutions); Penn, Temple, and Drexel participate in Yellow Ribbon at varying commitment levels [F11-SD2-02: Yellow Ribbon participation rates and per-student maximum at each PA-3 anchor institution; retrieve from Federal Student Aid Yellow Ribbon Program data].

**Institutional eligibility and accreditation.** HEA Title IV participation requires institutional accreditation by a recognized accreditor. PA-3 anchor institutions are accredited by the **Middle States Commission on Higher Education (MSCHE)** — the regional accreditor for the Mid-Atlantic. MSCHE accreditation is the gateway to Title IV participation; loss of accreditation terminates federal financial aid eligibility. *T11-SD2-02: Trump administration executive actions affecting accreditor oversight — ED released a regulatory agenda that could increase federal pressure on accreditors and institutional autonomy; trajectory is evolving as of May 2026.*

**Gainful Employment Rule** (34 C.F.R. Part 668): Promulgated by ED in 2023 under the Biden administration; establishes program-level accountability for whether graduates' earnings and debt loads meet specified thresholds. The Gainful Employment Rule's implementation status is temporally variable: *T11-SD2-03: Gainful Employment Rule implementation status under Trump administration — Biden-era GE Rule promulgated July 2023; implementation and enforcement posture under the Trump administration as of May 2026 is F-flagged pending ED regulatory status update [F11-SD2-03].*

### State Architecture

**Pennsylvania Higher Education Assistance Agency (PHEAA)**: PA's primary state student-aid agency. PHEAA administers the PA State Grant (need-based grant for PA residents; maximum amount approximately $3,000-$4,500/year for full-time students — *F11-SD2-04: current PHEAA PA State Grant maximum for 2025-26 award year; verify from PHEAA GrantUS platform*). PHEAA also administers Pennsylvania-specific loan programs and the American Education Services (AES) federal loan servicing portfolio. PHEAA's fiscal health has been under scrutiny given its declining servicing revenue as ED's federal loan servicing contracts shifted; *T11-SD2-04: PHEAA institutional fiscal condition; PHEAA contract status in federal loan servicing ecosystem.*

**State-related institution architecture.** Pennsylvania designates four institutions as "state-related" — receiving annual state appropriations from the General Assembly but operating as independent institutions rather than as state agencies: University of Pennsylvania, Temple University, University of Pittsburgh, and Lincoln University. The state-related appropriation is not formula-driven (unlike K-12 BEF); it is a line-item budget negotiation subject to the same legislative-impasse risks as all PA appropriations. *Statutory stability:* MEDIUM — appropriation amount is subject to annual legislative discretion; institutions cannot treat state-related funds as permanent baseline.

Temple University is the most PA-3-relevant state-related institution in terms of constituency-serving mission: Temple's North Philadelphia campus serves a predominantly urban student population with high Pell penetration and higher Black enrollment (29.7% in 2024, up from 20.9% [Source 21]) than at Penn. Temple's $200 million revenue loss from enrollment decline since 2017 and $60 million deficit requiring elimination of 190 positions (including approximately 50 layoffs) [Source 22] represents institutional financial distress with direct implications for the educational services and employment provided in PA-3's North Philadelphia geography.

Lincoln University — the nation's oldest HBCU, located in Chester County PA outside PA-3's boundaries but serving a significant PA-3 student population — is state-related and almost entirely dependent on federal student financial aid (approximately 100% of students rely on some form of federal aid [Source 22]). Lincoln's dependence on PHEAA state grant processing and ED financial-aid office operational capacity makes it acutely exposed to the federal capacity-reduction patterns T11-SD2-02 tracks.

**PA Community College Act** (24 P.S. § 19-1901-A et seq.): Governs Community College of Philadelphia's authorization. CCP is jointly sponsored by the City of Philadelphia and the Philadelphia County (coterminous) — with governance through a Board of Trustees and funding from three streams: tuition revenue; City of Philadelphia appropriation; Commonwealth community college appropriation. CCP's funding model creates similar fiscal dependency to SDP: flat public funding in an inflationary environment creates fiscal pressure addressed in March 2026 with CCP's first tuition increase in nine years [Source 20].

Penn's PILOET tax-exempt status is the D9 cross-reference anchor (see §3 cross-domain notes); Penn's role as the most active real-estate actor in West Philadelphia is the D7 SD1 cross-reference anchor. Enrollment peaked at approximately 40,000+ students before declining approximately 10,000 since Fall 2017 [Source 22]. Temple serves a high share of first-generation, low-income, and Black and Latino students relative to other research universities — its PA-3 educational-access mission is more direct than Penn's. Temple's enrollment and financial challenges are tracked at T11-SD2-05 (*Temple financial condition and enrollment trajectory under President John Fry's restructuring plan — including program eliminations and governance changes; updated annually*).

## Section 3: Pathway Tracing

**The Postsecondary Access Pathway in PA-3**

The representative postsecondary pathway for a PA-3 constituent begins at FAFSA completion — the access mechanism to all Title IV aid. FAFSA completion rates in lower-income urban districts like SDP's feeder population are systematically lower than in higher-income communities, producing a structural access barrier that precedes any enrollment decision. A PA-3 high school senior who does not complete FAFSA is categorically excluded from Pell Grant, Direct Loan, and PHEAA State Grant eligibility; institutions cannot provide Title IV aid absent FAFSA data. *F11-SD2-05: Philadelphia-specific FAFSA completion rate for SDP graduates — retrieve from National College Access Network or ED College Scorecard locality data; compare to state and national rates.*

*Institutional-choice layer.* The PA-3 postsecondary landscape stratifies by cost-and-access across clear tiers: CCP (highest-access; lowest-cost; 60% Pell); Temple (state-related; urban-serving; moderate cost; high Pell penetration); Drexel (private; co-op model; high sticker price; moderate-to-high merit aid); Penn (Ivy; very high sticker price; very high need-based aid for enrolled low-income students; extremely low admittance rate). The structural gap: the institutions most accessible to low-income PA-3 constituents (CCP; Temple) are the institutions under the greatest fiscal stress; the institutions with the most resources for need-based aid (Penn) are the least accessible by admissions selectivity. The both-sides-of-the-resource-gap dynamic is SD2's central structural finding.

*Both/And (HEA Title IV institutional access — substantive):* Pell Grants fund real access for CCP's 60% Pell recipients and for Temple's high-Pell undergraduate population. The substantive access program works for enrolled students.

*Both/And (HEA Title IV institutional access — structural gap):* Cohort default rates at community colleges and less-selective institutions are structurally higher than at selective four-year institutions, because the economic vulnerability of the student population is higher and completion rates are lower. Students who borrow and do not complete face the worst-of-both-worlds outcome: debt without credential. *F11-SD2-06: CCP and Temple cohort default rates for the most recent three-year CDR period; retrieve from Federal Student Aid CDR data.*

*D10 SD7 boundary — postsecondary credentials:* The D10 SD7 cross-reference protocol (per D10 verified file boundary call) establishes that postsecondary workforce credentials at CCP and Title IX institutional compliance at anchor institutions are D11 SD2 primary territory with a D10 SD7 cross-reference for the labor-market outcome dimension. SD2 respects this boundary: workforce-pathway architecture is D10 SD7; educational-institutional architecture is D11 SD2.

**Anchor-Engaged Community-Engagement Educational Programs (Both/And)**

Penn, Temple, Drexel, and Jefferson each operate community-engagement educational programs directed at PA-3 constituents: Penn's Netter Center for Community Partnerships (university-assisted community schools; course-based community service); Temple's community education programming; Drexel's Dornsife Center for Neighborhood Partnerships (West Philadelphia); Jefferson's community health professions pipeline programs. The substructure §6 Both/And designation (reclassified from HOM candidate) applies: these programs serve genuine community educational goals AND scale-vs-need questions remain open. ## Section 2: Authority Chain

### Constitutional Foundation

There is no federal constitutional right to early childhood education. The federal early childhood programs rest on the **Spending Clause** (conditional grants: Head Start; CCDBG CCDF). PA's **Art. III § 14** Education Clause has been interpreted to guarantee a "thorough and efficient" system for school-age children (William Penn ruling); pre-K is not constitutionally mandated at the state level. The constitutional absence of a pre-K entitlement means that early childhood coverage levels are determined entirely by appropriation: federal Head Start funding; PA Pre-K Counts annual budget allocation; Philadelphia Beverage Tax revenue supporting PHLpreK.

### Federal Architecture

**Child Care and Development Block Grant Act of 1990 as amended (CCDBG)**, 42 U.S.C. § 9858 et seq. (Pub. L. 113-186, reauthorized 2014). Provides federal block grant funding to states for child care subsidies (Child Care Works in PA) and quality improvement activities (Keystone STARS in PA). The CCDBG funds the child care subsidy side — helping low-income working parents pay for child care — rather than funding educational programs directly. D12 SD6 owns the CCDBG subsidy eligibility architecture; D11 SD3 notes CCDBG as the federal baseline authorizing state child care quality systems (Keystone STARS quality rating system) that set the floor for program quality in which PHLpreK and Pre-K Counts providers operate.

**Head Start Act**, 42 U.S.C. § 9831 et seq. (originally Economic Opportunity Act of 1964; current authorization P.L. 110-134, Improving Head Start for School Readiness Act of 2007). Head Start is a direct federal grant program administered by the HHS Administration for Children and Families (ACF) through local grantees. Head Start provides comprehensive early childhood education, health, nutrition, and family support services to children from birth to age five in families at or below the federal poverty level. Early Head Start (EHS) serves families from birth through age three. In Philadelphia, Head Start grantees include Community Behavioral Health, multiple community-based organizations, and SDP-operated Head Start programs. *F11-SD3-01: current Head Start enrollment in Philadelphia / PA-3 geography — retrieve from HHS ACF Head Start Program Information Report (PIR) for 2023-24.* Statutory stability: HIGH; Head Start is durable federal programming. *T11-SD3-01: Trump administration ACF capacity and Head Start funding posture — ACF staffing reductions under the Trump administration (related to D3 TC-06 SAMHSA pattern; analogous capacity erosion) warrant monitoring for Head Start grantee oversight capacity.*

*Verification update (2026-05-10): T11-SD3-01 resolved with partial quantification. ACF administrative events confirmed: (a) January 2025 — funding freeze issued; reversed after 23-state AG lawsuit and federal court TRO. (b) March 2025 — 30-45% ACF staff reduction-in-force announced. (c) April 1, 2025 — 5 of 10 ACF regional offices closed: Boston, Chicago, New York, San Francisco, Seattle. Philadelphia ACF regional office REMAINS OPEN — Head Start grantee oversight capacity for PA-3 is comparatively less disrupted than regions where offices closed. (d) FY2026 continuing resolution (March 14, 2025) — $750M in cuts to Head Start programs. (e) Trump FY2026 budget proposal (May 2025) — Head Start NOT eliminated; program level-funded at $12.27 billion. (f) PA legislative response — PA House passed H.B. 1505 (July 2025) authorizing state Pre-K Counts appropriation as backstop if federal Head Start funding is eliminated. Administrative vulnerability: CONFIRMED ELEVATED — the freeze, office closures, and funding cuts created grantee uncertainty; payment delays documented nationally (states received 34% less in Jan-Apr 2025 vs. Jan-Apr 2024 despite level funding). Program survived FY2026 elimination threat but grantee oversight capacity diminished in 5 of 10 regions. See TC-03 (temporal-changes sidecar). See MC-07.*

### State Architecture

**PA Pre-K Counts**, administered by the Pennsylvania Department of Education (PDE) under authority of the PA School Code (24 P.S. § 15-1533 et seq. and appropriation acts). Pre-K Counts funds high-quality, part-day pre-K for three- and four-year-old children in families with household income below 300% of the Federal Poverty Guidelines. Programs must be Keystone STAR 3 or STAR 4 rated to qualify. Pre-K Counts providers include school districts, Head Start programs, child care centers, and nursery schools meeting the quality standards. The program is entirely appropriation-dependent: PA Pre-K Counts funding levels are set annually in the state budget, with no constitutional mandate requiring any specific coverage level. *F11-SD3-02: PA Pre-K Counts 2024-25 and 2025-26 annual appropriation levels and statewide enrollment served — retrieve from PDE Bureau of Early Learning or PA Office of Child Development and Early Learning (OCDEL) annual report.* The William Penn ruling's post-2023 adequacy-formula investment has not directly addressed pre-K Counts — it applies to K-12 schooling; pre-K Counts appropriation levels are a separate legislative product. *T11-SD3-02: PA Pre-K Counts appropriation trajectory in post-William-Penn state budget cycles — whether adequacy-focused state budget investments are paired with Pre-K Counts expansion.*

**PA Office of Child Development and Early Learning (OCDEL)**, jointly administered by PDE and the PA Department of Human Services. OCDEL oversees the Keystone STARS quality improvement system (statewide rating and improvement system for early childhood programs), the Early Intervention program (cross-reference SD4 for IDEA Part C early intervention), and the statewide coordination of child care quality with federal CCDBG compliance.

### Local Architecture

**PHLpreK**, administered by the City of Philadelphia Mayor's Office of Children and Families (OCF). PHLpreK provides free, quality pre-K to children ages three and four residing in Philadelphia, with no income requirements [Source 24]. PHLpreK is funded by the Philadelphia Beverage Tax (the "soda tax" enacted 2017; 1.5 cents per ounce on sweetened beverages). PHLpreK operates through a network of community-based and school-based providers that meet quality standards; providers receive per-child funding from OCF. For the 2024-25 school year, PHLpreK offered 5,250 seats across 228 programs [Source 23]. The universal application (launched for 2025-26 enrollment) allows families to apply for PHLpreK, PA Pre-K Counts, and SDP pre-K in a single process [Source 23].

The Beverage Tax revenue is structurally variable: a one-cent-per-ounce tax subject to consumption patterns, litigation challenges (litigation was resolved in favor of the tax in PA Supreme Court), and economic conditions. PHLpreK's seat capacity is therefore a function of Beverage Tax revenue, which is not a stable appropriation. *T11-SD3-03: Philadelphia Beverage Tax revenue trajectory and PHLpreK seat capacity — annual Beverage Tax receipts and PHLpreK seat expansion or contraction; retrieve from City of Philadelphia Finance Department or OCF annual report.*

**SDP Pre-K programs**: SDP operates pre-K programs (using federal Head Start funding; PA Pre-K Counts; and state Ready to Learn funding) serving three- and four-year-olds in school buildings. SDP's pre-K is part of the universal application for 2025-26.

## Section 6: Conversational Narrative Note

The early childhood architecture in Philadelphia is layered but not integrated. PHLpreK (income-universal, Beverage Tax-funded), PA Pre-K Counts (income-limited, state-appropriation-funded), and Head Start (income-limited, federal-grant-funded) each serve different slices of the population through separate administrative systems, with a universal application partially reducing navigation friction beginning 2025-26. The three programs together still do not serve all eligible three-and-four-year-olds in Philadelphia; the coverage gap is the primary structural finding.

The Beverage Tax architecture for PHLpreK is Philadelphia's most innovative local-tax-funded educational program, but its structural dependency on a consumption tax makes the seat capacity inherently variable. The tax has survived legal challenge, but its revenue base is subject to behavioral change (reduced consumption), economic downturns, and political reconsideration across mayoral administrations. A future administration's decision to redirect Beverage Tax revenue away from PHLpreK would eliminate the universal-access layer without constitutional or statutory protection.

D11-Thread A's federal-floor-on-state-system analysis reaches its earliest-pipeline expression at SD3: Head Start's federal floor establishes a minimum quality standard for the lowest-income population; PA Pre-K Counts' state appropriation layer reaches income-moderate families above Head Start's threshold; PHLpreK's local layer provides income-universal access. But the floor-supplementation-and-local-expansion architecture does not produce universal coverage — it produces a patchwork with specific gaps that correlate with program availability, geographic distribution, and administrative-navigation capacity.

## Section 2: Authority Chain

### Federal Architecture — Special Education

**Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA)**, 20 U.S.C. § 1400 et seq.

SD1 introduced IDEA at the institutional-set level. SD4 is the PRIMARY engagement. IDEA Part B (20 U.S.C. § 1412) governs special education for children ages 3-21. Core requirements:
- *Free Appropriate Public Education (FAPE):* Every IDEA-eligible student is entitled to specially designed instruction and related services at public expense, meeting the student's unique needs, designed to confer meaningful educational benefit.
- *Least Restrictive Environment (LRE):* Placement decisions must maximize the extent appropriate the student's education with non-disabled peers (20 U.S.C. § 1412(a)(5)).
- *Individualized Education Program (IEP):* Each IDEA-eligible student must have a written IEP developed by a team including parents, regular education teachers, special education teachers, and school administrators (20 U.S.C. § 1414(d)).
- *Child Find:* LEAs are affirmatively obligated to identify all children with disabilities who may be in need of special education and related services (20 U.S.C. § 1412(a)(3)). SDP's child-find obligation extends to public charter LEAs operating in Philadelphia; charter LEAs authorized by SDP are covered.
- *Procedural Safeguards:* Parental rights to notice, consent, and due process (hearing rights; mediation; state complaint process) (20 U.S.C. § 1415).

Implementing federal regulations: 34 C.F.R. Part 300 (IDEA Part B). Implementing PA regulations: 22 Pa. Code Ch. 14 (the "Chapter 14 regs" — PA supplements the federal IDEA floor in several respects). *Statutory stability:* HIGH at the framework level; IDEA entitlement architecture is durable federal law.

**IDEA Part C** (20 U.S.C. § 1431-1444): Early Intervention for infants and toddlers with disabilities (birth through age two). Administered in PA through PA OCDEL. Part C → Part B transition at age three (§ 1437(a)(9) — transition planning requirements) is the bridge from SD3 territory (early childhood) to SD4 IDEA Part B.

**Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973**, 29 U.S.C. § 794: Prohibits disability discrimination by recipients of federal financial assistance. In K-12 education, Section 504 covers students who have a physical or mental impairment substantially limiting a major life activity but who may not meet the IDEA eligibility criteria for special education. Section 504 plans (accommodation plans, distinct from IEPs) require LEA compliance without the formula-grant structure of IDEA.

*Both/And discipline at Section 504:* Section 504's eligibility threshold is lower than IDEA's (fewer categories; broader functional-limitation standard); the Section 504 protection exists AND the implementation burden on the student/family (no formula grant to the LEA for Section 504 compliance; self-identification; request-driven accommodation) creates access barriers relative to IDEA's affirmative child-find obligation.

*D3 SD4 cross-reference:* IDEA identification + racial disparity in placement (G1-SD4-06 — IDEA-ED placement framework + racial disparity per D3_mentalHealth_verified_REMEDIATED) is the substantive cross-reference to D3's pediatric behavioral health findings. The racial disparity in IDEA emotional disturbance classification — where Black students are over-identified in high-restriction categories and under-identified in autism and specific-learning-disability categories relative to their white peers — is documented nationally through ED OCR CRDC data [Source 27].

### Federal Architecture — Homeless Education

**McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act, Title VII — Education for Homeless Children and Youth**, 42 U.S.C. § 11431 et seq.

SD4 is the PRIMARY D11 engagement for McKinney-Vento Title VII. The MC19 CARRY-FORWARD from D7 SD6 G7-SD6-03 governs the primary analytical content:

*MC19 definitional gap:* The Title I (HUD/CoC) definition of homelessness (40 U.S.C. § 11302) excludes doubled-up households (families staying temporarily with others due to housing loss or economic hardship). The Title VII (education) definition of homeless children and youth (42 U.S.C. § 11434a(2)) explicitly includes children in doubled-up situations. Families doubled up are therefore counted as homeless for educational services eligibility (McKinney-Vento Title VII) but not for CoC housing services eligibility (McKinney-Vento Title I / HUD). PIT counts and HMIS data — which operate under the Title I definition — systematically undercount housing-instability prevalence relevant to education-system demand; the denominator for educational-service need is larger than the denominator for housing-service need, but resource-allocation decisions for housing often set the referral context for educational services [D7 SD6 G7-SD6-03; Source 11].

*Title VII operational requirements:* Immediate enrollment without prior school records, immunization documentation, or proof of residency (42 U.S.C. § 11432(g)(3)(C)). Transportation to the school of origin if in the student's best interest (42 U.S.C. § 11432(g)(1)(J)). SDP is required to have a local homeless liaison who identifies McKinney-Vento-eligible students and coordinates services.

*IDEA intersection:* McKinney-Vento-eligible students who also have IEPs face a compound service-delivery problem: transportation to the school of origin for McKinney-Vento purposes may conflict with IDEA LRE placement decisions; IEP service continuity across residential moves requires expedited record transfer; homeless students with disabilities are at higher risk of service disruption than either population independently.

### Federal Architecture — English Language Learner

**Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964**, 42 U.S.C. § 2000d: Prohibits national-origin discrimination in programs receiving federal financial assistance; interpreted by ED OCR to require affirmative steps to provide meaningful language access to English language learners.

**Lau v. Nichols**, 414 U.S. 563 (1974): Supreme Court held that failing to provide English language learner students with language access support constitutes national-origin discrimination under Title VI. Lau compliance requires LEAs to identify ELL students, assess language proficiency, and provide language instruction programs (English as a Second Language; bilingual education).

**Equal Educational Opportunities Act (EEOA) of 1974**, 20 U.S.C. § 1703(f): Requires school districts to take "appropriate action to overcome language barriers that impede equal participation by its students in its instructional programs."

**SDP ELL architecture:** SDP's Office of Multilingual Curriculum and Programs administers the ELL identification and programming architecture. SDP uses the World-class Instructional Design and Assessment (WIDA) English proficiency screener for identification. SDP's ELL population has grown substantially; as of 2024-25, Spanish remains the most common non-English home language; Portuguese has grown rapidly (second after Spanish; surpassing Mandarin since 2021-22) [Source 1]. *F11-SD4-01: current SDP ELL enrollment by language group; retrieve from SDP Office of Research and Evaluation; SDP primary enrollment data.*

### Federal Architecture — Title IX (K-12)

**Title IX**, 20 U.S.C. § 1681: SD4 primary engagement for K-12 Title IX. SD2 noted Title IX at postsecondary institutions. K-12 Title IX prohibits sex discrimination in educational programs receiving federal financial assistance; covers student-on-student sexual harassment under the deliberate-indifference standard (post-*Davis v. Monroe County Board of Education*, 526 U.S. 629 (1999)); gender-based harassment; and sex-based disciplinary disparities. *T11-SD4-01: Trump administration Title IX rulemaking — Biden administration's 2024 Final Rule (substantially expanding Title IX protections including gender identity; 34 C.F.R. Part 106) was challenged in multiple federal courts; multiple injunctions issued; implementation status is temporally variable as of May 2026. The 2020 Trump-era Rule remains the operational baseline in jurisdictions where the 2024 Rule is enjoined. F11-SD4-02: current operational status of 2024 Title IX Final Rule as applied to Pennsylvania and SDP.*

### Federal Architecture — ED Office for Civil Rights

**ED Office for Civil Rights (OCR)**: Investigates complaints alleging violations of Title VI, Title IX, Section 504, and the Age Discrimination Act at educational institutions receiving federal financial assistance. OCR enforces through complaint resolution (requiring compliance commitments from investigated institutions), voluntary resolution agreements, and, in cases of continued non-compliance, referral for fund termination.

**Civil Rights Data Collection (CRDC)**: ED OCR's biennial data collection from all public schools and school districts on discipline, access, and outcomes disaggregated by race, disability status, sex, and ELL status. CRDC is the primary national dataset for documenting educational civil-rights gaps; SDP submits CRDC data. *Standard 11 administrative-vulnerability:* ED OCR enforcement capacity and complaint-processing timelines are highly variable by administration. The Trump administration's track record of rolling back Obama-era Title IX guidance and reducing OCR staff is documented; any significant reduction in OCR capacity has direct consequences for SDP students with civil-rights complaints. *T11-SD4-02: ED OCR staffing and enforcement posture under Trump administration — monitoring for staffing reductions analogous to the D3 SAMHSA capacity erosion pattern (TC-06); any executive direction reducing OCR complaint investigation scope.*

### State Architecture

**PA Special Education Regulations**, 22 Pa. Code Ch. 14: PA's Chapter 14 regs supplement IDEA Part B in several respects, including additional procedural timelines, extended age range requirements, and specific provisions for gifted education that do not have a federal analog. SDP's special education compliance is governed by Chapter 14 plus the federal Part 300 regulations.

**PA Safe Schools Report**: Annual report by the PA Office of Safe Schools documenting school discipline data (suspensions; expulsions; incidents) by LEA. The Safe Schools Report is the PA-primary data source for discipline patterns prior to the CRDC reporting cycle.

## Section 3: Pathway Tracing

**Pathway A: K-12 Student with Disability — IDEA IEP Pathway**

*Triggering condition:* PA-3 K-12 student whose parents or teacher identifies possible developmental, learning, or behavioral needs.

*Child-find entry:* SDP's affirmative child-find obligation means SDP must evaluate when there is reason to suspect disability. The evaluation timeline (34 C.F.R. § 300.301: 60-day evaluation timeline from parental consent in PA) produces the Initial Evaluation; the IEP team convenes; eligibility is determined across 14 IDEA disability categories.

*Identification disparity:* The identification process is where the Both/And structural gap operates. Black students nationally are over-identified in the "emotional disturbance" category (high-restriction placement) and under-identified in "autism" and "specific learning disability" relative to their white peers [Source 27; D3 SD4 cross-reference for the racial-disparity pattern]. In SDP, where Black students constitute approximately 50%+ of enrollment, the identification pattern shapes the distribution of students across placements — from inclusion in general education (LRE) to substantially separate special education programs.

*IEP → placement → service delivery:* Once IEP-eligible, the student receives a placement in the LRE continuum (full inclusion; resource room; substantially separate classroom; special education school). Service delivery quality depends on SDP's special education staffing — which faces documented teacher shortage in special education nationally and in urban districts specifically. *F11-SD4-03: SDP special education teacher vacancy rate; retrieve from SDP Office of Specialized Services or PA Educator Pipeline data.*

*Section 504 pathway:* Students not meeting IDEA eligibility may qualify for Section 504 plans. Unlike IDEA (parent-notification trigger; child-find obligation), Section 504 in practice is more request-driven. Section 504 plan development and monitoring is less formalized than IEP; compliance oversight is through ED OCR rather than IDEA's state performance plan / annual performance report structure.

**Pathway B: Homeless Student — McKinney-Vento Title VII Pathway**

*Triggering condition:* PA-3 family loses housing stability (eviction; domestic violence displacement; doubled-up arrangement; transitional housing; shelter).

*McKinney-Vento identification:* SDP's homeless liaison is responsible for identifying McKinney-Vento-eligible students at enrollment and through existing-student monitoring. Identification relies on staff training, intake questionnaires, and liaison outreach. The definitional gap (MC19): families doubled-up are McKinney-Vento-eligible for Title VII purposes; SDP's liaison should identify them. In practice, doubled-up families may not disclose housing instability on school forms [F11-SD4-04: SDP McKinney-Vento-identified student count for 2023-24; retrieve from SDP Office of Student Support Services or PA annual Title VII report to ED].

*School of origin / enrollment:* McKinney-Vento requires immediate enrollment without records; the school of origin presumption (staying in the school where the student was enrolled before housing instability) protects educational continuity. Transportation to the school of origin is required if in the student's best interest. Transportation funding is a structural constraint [SD7 territory for transportation funding-stream; SD4 notes the architecture].

**Pathway C: ELL Student — Language Access Pathway**

*Triggering condition:* Non-English-speaking or limited-English-proficient student enrolls in SDP.

*WIDA screening:* SDP administers the WIDA ACCESS screener to identify ELL status. Identified ELL students must receive English language instruction (ESL or bilingual programs) appropriate to their proficiency level.

*Language access gap:* SDP's rapidly growing Portuguese-speaking population (surpassing Mandarin as second-largest non-English group [Source 1]) has outpaced SDP's bilingual staffing capacity in Portuguese. ## Section 6: Conversational Narrative Note

SD4's Both/And operates differently from SD1's MC49/MC50/MC51 within-domain-analytical-tension pattern. The SD1 Both/Ands each involve a program simultaneously serving constituents and producing structural displacement or revenue diversion. SD4's Both/And — IDEA + Section 504 entitlement architecture serving students with disabilities AND structural under-identification + differential-discipline patterns — is an entitlement architecture that serves constituents AND is implemented through a process (identification) that produces racially disparate outcomes in violation of the equitable intent the entitlement was designed to advance.

The identification disparity is not an artifact of bad intentions — it is a structural output of a system in which implicit bias, resource constraints (limited bilingual special education evaluators; limited culturally responsive assessment tools), and disciplinary culture interact at the identification decision point. The IDEA entitlement cannot fix the identification disparity by design — it applies after identification. The gap between IDEA's formal equity commitment and the racially patterned identification outcomes it produces is D11-Thread C at SD4: the substantive entitlement program contributes to the structural inequity it was designed to remedy.

The MC19 McKinney-Vento definitional gap is the SD4 finding with the most direct connection to the project's cumulative-disadvantage-chain framing. Doubled-up families — the housing-stability situation that is most invisible to the housing-services system (no CoC eligibility) while being visible to the education system (McKinney-Vento Title VII eligible) — are precisely the families most likely to be in the middle of D7's housing-instability spiral (post-eviction; pre-shelter; financially stressed). The education system's broader definitional aperture means the schools see these families before the housing system does; the schools' McKinney-Vento obligations are therefore the most upstream intervention point in the housing-instability-to-educational-disruption chain.

## Section 7: Gap Analysis

**G11-SD4-01: IDEA racial identification disparity — over-identification in high-restriction categories; under-identification in other categories (BOTH/AND STRUCTURAL GAP)**
*Finding:* Black students nationally are over-identified in the IDEA "emotional disturbance" (ED) category — a high-restriction placement category — and under-identified in "autism" and "specific learning disability" relative to their white peers [Source 27; D3 SD4 G1-SD4-06]. The SDP-specific disparity is F-flagged [F11-SD4-06]; the national pattern is documented through ED OCR CRDC data (Source 27). The structural gap is that the IDEA entitlement architecture that was designed to serve students with disabilities produces categorization outcomes that are racially patterned and that correlate with educational-trajectory harm (high-restriction placement; suspension exposure; reduced mainstream course access).
*Evidence type:* [D] for national CRDC pattern; [SI] for SDP-specific magnitude pending F11-SD4-06.
*Confidence:* HIGH for structural pattern; MEDIUM for SDP magnitude.

**G11-SD4-02: MC19 McKinney-Vento definitional gap — doubled-up households underserved (CARRY-FORWARD from D7 SD6 G7-SD6-03)**
*Finding:* The Title I / Title VII definitional gap means families in doubled-up housing situations are McKinney-Vento Title VII-eligible for educational services but not Title I-eligible for CoC housing services. PIT counts and HMIS data — operating under the Title I definition — systematically undercount housing-instability prevalence relevant to the education system. SDP's McKinney-Vento-identified population is therefore likely undercounted, as the identification process depends partly on family self-disclosure and liaison outreach rather than a referral pipeline from the housing-services system that formally excludes these families [D7 SD6 G7-SD6-03; Source 11].
*Evidence type:* [D] for definitional gap; [SI] for identification undercounting magnitude.

## Section 1: Header and Regime Declaration

**PA-3 stakes framing.** Adult basic education is the remedial ramp connecting adults who did not complete or who completed insufficient K-12 education to the labor market and to postsecondary pathways. D11-Thread A (federal-floor-on-state-system) operates at SD5: WIOA Title II sets the federal minimum; PA Bureau of ABLE supplements; local providers deliver under state contracts; the three-layer stack does not produce universal adult literacy coverage in PA-3.

## Section 2: Authority Chain

### Federal Architecture

**WIOA Title II — Adult Education and Family Literacy Act (AEFLA)**, 29 U.S.C. § 3271 et seq. (Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act, P.L. 113-128, July 2014). Title II authorizes formula grants to states for adult education and literacy activities. Key distinctions from HEA Title IV (SD2):
- *Administering office:* OCTAE (Office of Career, Technical, and Adult Education) within the U.S. Department of Education — not FSA (Federal Student Aid) or OESE. OCTAE administers Title II alongside Perkins V (SD6) as the CTE/adult-ed branch of ED.
- *Eligible activities:* Adult basic education (ABE — below 8th grade literacy); adult secondary education (ASE — 9th-12th grade equivalent; GED preparation; HiSET); English language acquisition programs (ELA); integrated education and training (IET — combines literacy instruction with workforce training); family literacy (Even Start model).
- *Eligibility:* Adults 16 and older who are not enrolled in secondary school and lack secondary credentials or basic literacy skills. No income eligibility threshold (unlike Head Start; Pell eligibility does not apply to WIOA Title II programs directly).
- *State formula:* WIOA Title II distributes funds to states; states subgrant to eligible providers (school districts; community organizations; libraries; community colleges) through a competitive grant process. PA receives a WIOA Title II formula allocation; PA Bureau of ABLE subgrants to local providers.

*Boundary with WIOA Title I-B (D10 SD7 primary):* WIOA Title I-B funds adult employment services (One-Stop/PA CareerLink; employment and training activities for adult workers). Title I-B and Title II are designed to be co-located at One-Stop Career Centers (PA CareerLink). The integration between Title II adult literacy instruction and Title I-B employment services is a design feature of WIOA's unified workforce development system — and a source of structural tension when adult learners need literacy instruction before they can access Title I-B employment training.

*Statutory stability:* HIGH at the framework level; WIOA Title II was last reauthorized in 2014; reauthorization has been pending but the program continues under continuing appropriations. *T11-SD5-01: WIOA reauthorization status — WIOA authorization lapsed; continuing appropriations maintain Title II; any enacted reauthorization in 2025-26 would update the authority chain.*

### State Architecture

**PA Bureau of Adult Basic and Literacy Education (ABLE)**, within the PA Department of Education. PA Bureau of ABLE administers the WIOA Title II formula grant in Pennsylvania, operates the state's adult education data system (PALS — Pennsylvania's Adult Education Reporting System), and subgrants to local adult education providers. PA Bureau of ABLE also administers the High School Equivalency Assessment program (GED; HiSET) through which PA residents obtain high school equivalency credentials. *F11-SD5-01: PA WIOA Title II allocation for 2024-25 and statewide enrollment served; retrieve from PA Bureau of ABLE annual report or OCTAE state profile data.*

### Local Architecture

**Community-based adult education providers**: Philadelphia hosts a network of WIOA Title II-funded adult education providers. Key institutional actors:
- **Community Learning Center (CLC)**: Philadelphia's primary adult education provider; operated by SDP; serves adults in GED preparation, English literacy, and basic skills programs. CLC is the largest adult education provider in Philadelphia [F11-SD5-02: CLC enrollment for 2024-25; retrieve from CLC or SDP reports].
- **New Foundations Charter School Adult Education**: Charter-sector adult education offering GED preparation.
- **Community organizations**: Philadelphia-based workforce development and adult education nonprofits (e.g., Philadelphia Youth Network; Congreso de Latinos Unidos; The Bridge Network) operate adult literacy and ESL programs funded through WIOA Title II and private foundation grants.
- **Free Library of Philadelphia**: The Free Library operates adult literacy programs as a WIOA Title II-funded local library adult education resource. The Free Library's network of branch libraries extends adult education access across all four PA-3 sub-areas; the library is a low-barrier, geographically distributed access point for adult learners not connected to formal adult education programs.
- **CCP Continuing Education**: CCP's division of continuing education offers non-credit workforce and English language courses that parallel WIOA Title II programming — creating an institutional interface between SD5 (adult basic education) and SD2 (postsecondary) for adults seeking credential pathways.

*Within-D11 boundary:* D11-originating adult education workforce pathways (adults seeking K-12 credentials or adult literacy through D11 SD5 programs as a gateway to postsecondary enrollment at CCP or career training) live at SD5 with a within-D11 cross-reference to SD2 (CCP) and SD6 (CTE programs). D10-originating workforce pathway analysis (Philadelphia Works; PA CareerLink co-location; workforce training programs) stays at D10 SD7 primary with a D11 SD5 cross-reference.

## Section 3: Pathway Tracing

**The Adult Basic Education Pathway in PA-3**

*Triggering condition:* PA-3 adult without a high school diploma or high school equivalency credential, or with limited English proficiency, seeking to improve educational attainment.

*Entry point:* PA CareerLink (One-Stop center) or direct enrollment with a WIOA Title II provider (CLC; Free Library; community organization). The WIOA unified system design places adult education providers (Title II) co-located with employment services (Title I-B) at One-Stop Career Centers, enabling seamless referral between literacy instruction and employment training. In practice, co-location discipline varies; providers may operate in separate facilities.

*Assessment:* WIOA Title II requires educational functioning level (EFL) assessment for enrolled adults using approved assessments (CASAS; TABE; BEST Plus for ELA). Assessment places adults in the appropriate program level (ABE; ASE; ELA).

*Integrated Education and Training (IET) pathway:* WIOA Title II's IET model combines adult literacy instruction with occupational skills training, allowing adults to simultaneously work toward literacy and workforce credentials. IET aligns directly with the D10 SD7 workforce-pathway architecture (community college occupational training; registered apprenticeship pipeline at D11 SD6). IET is the bridge mechanism from adult education to the labor market for adults who cannot access HEA Title IV financial aid without first obtaining a high school equivalency credential.

*GED/HiSET completion:* Successful completion of the HiSET or GED qualifies the adult for HEA Title IV financial aid eligibility, opening CCP enrollment (SD2 pathway) and Pell Grant access.

## Section 6: Conversational Narrative Note

Adult basic education is the domain where the K-12 achievement gap of one generation becomes the adult literacy gap of the next. The SDP's goal of moving K-12 reading proficiency from 34% to 65% by 2030 — an ambitious target — will require fifteen years to produce adults who completed K-12 schooling at the target proficiency level. D11-Thread A at SD5 has the clearest federal-floor-limitation pattern: WIOA Title II's formula allocation to Pennsylvania, and PA Bureau of ABLE's subgrant to Philadelphia providers, sets the capacity ceiling that determines how many adults CLC can serve, how many evening GED preparation slots exist, and how long waitlists run. ### Federal Architecture

**Strengthening Career and Technical Education for the 21st Century Act (Perkins V)**, 20 U.S.C. § 2301 et seq. (P.L. 115-224, July 2018). Perkins V authorizes formula grants to states for secondary and postsecondary CTE programs. Key provisions:
- States submit a four-year State Plan to DOE OCTAE; plans must include accountability measures and program improvement strategies.
- States distribute the majority of funds (85%) to eligible recipients (local educational agencies operating secondary CTE programs; postsecondary institutions).
- Perkins V requires alignment between CTE programs and in-demand industry sectors; emphasizes work-based learning opportunities (internships; apprenticeships; cooperative education).
- Special populations: Perkins V specifically requires equitable access for individuals with disabilities, ELL students, foster-care-involved students, and members of racial/ethnic minority groups. Performance accountability disaggregated by special population is a Perkins V requirement.
- *Statutory stability:* HIGH; Perkins V was enacted 2018 and represents the most recent CTE reauthorization.

**National Apprenticeship Act**, 29 U.S.C. § 50 (1937, administered by DOL Office of Apprenticeship). Registered apprenticeship programs are D10 SD7 primary territory. SD6 engages the apprenticeship-classroom interface: how CTE secondary and postsecondary programs (Perkins V-funded) connect students to DOL-registered apprenticeship programs. The "pre-apprenticeship" model — where CTE programs provide foundational skills that facilitate entry into registered apprenticeship — is the SD6 cross-reference mechanism to D10 SD7.

**PA Statewide Articulation Agreements**: PA has developed articulation agreements between secondary CTE programs and postsecondary institutions (CCP; PASSHE institutions) to grant college credit for completed CTE coursework — reducing barriers to postsecondary credential attainment for CTE completers.

**MC48 Emergent-From-Interaction HOM — Contributing Mechanism Enumeration**

The school-to-prison pipeline as emergent phenomenon arises from the combined operation of multiple architectures. SD7 enumerates contributing mechanisms without ranking:

**Mechanism 1: School discipline architecture — suspension and expulsion cascade.** PA Safe Schools Act establishes mandatory expulsion for certain categories of offenses (weapons; controlled substances); SDP's discipline code extends beyond mandatory categories to a broader range of conduct subject to disciplinary action. Out-of-school suspension (OSS) and expulsion remove students from educational contact — directly reducing educational attainment — and correlate with subsequent criminal-justice involvement. The removal-from-school → increased criminal-justice-contact pathway is documented nationally; the causal mechanism is not established with precision at SD7 (both directions of causality are plausible; held-open per HOM discipline). SDP's PBIS initiative is designed to interrupt this pathway; SDP enacted a policy eliminating K-2 suspensions in 2017. Despite these reforms, SDP OSS remains racially disparate [Sources 25, 26, 27; F11-SD4-06 from SD4 for current CRDC figures].

**Mechanism 2: Title VI / Section 504 / IDEA differential application in school discipline.** Black students are suspended at higher rates than white students for similar or ambiguous conduct; students with disabilities (particularly IDEA emotional-disturbance-classified students; SD4) face elevated discipline rates; the intersection of race and disability produces compounding vulnerability. The differential application of discipline across race and disability categories is one mechanism through which the school safety architecture (designed to protect all students) produces structurally disparate outcomes. This is the SD4 contributing-mechanism cluster carried forward to SD7 for the emergent-HOM mechanism inventory.

**Mechanism 3: Truancy enforcement — mandatory attendance → juvenile court pipeline.** Pennsylvania's compulsory attendance law (24 P.S. § 13-1326) triggers truancy enforcement proceedings when students accumulate unauthorized absences. The truancy enforcement pipeline — school attendance officer; District Justice; juvenile court — introduces judicial involvement into what begins as an educational-attendance problem. Juvenile court adjudication for truancy can produce probation terms, detention hearings, and a juvenile record — each a ratchet step toward deeper criminal-justice involvement. The truancy pipeline is more commonly engaged for students in lower-income neighborhoods (higher chronic absenteeism correlated with housing instability, health needs, and family economic stress), which is itself correlated with race in PA-3's documented geography.

**Mechanism 4: School Resource Officer (SRO) presence and mandatory referral provisions.** SROs are sworn Philadelphia Police Department officers deployed in SDP schools under the SDP-PPD MOU. SRO presence in schools creates a police-jurisdiction layer within the educational environment. Mandatory referral provisions (specific conduct categories that trigger SRO involvement rather than administrative discipline) transfer disciplinary decisions from educational administrators (who can impose school-based consequences) to law-enforcement officers (who can issue citations, make arrests, and generate criminal/juvenile justice records). The expansion of SRO presence and mandatory referral has been documented nationally as a driver of school-based arrests; the SDP-PPD MOU's mandatory referral parameters are a load-bearing element of this mechanism [F11-SD7-02 for the current MOU parameters]. *Cross-reference D17 (planned; Public Safety / Police): D17 will own the PPD-side analysis of SRO deployment and police-school interface; SD7 owns the school-side architecture.*

**Mechanism 5: Criminal-justice involvement — school-based arrests and juvenile justice entry.** School-based arrests (distinct from SRO presence) introduce students to the formal criminal or juvenile justice system through school-building contact. ED OCR's CRDC collects referrals-to-law-enforcement and school-related-arrests data; national data shows Black students are referred to law enforcement and arrested at school at disproportionately higher rates than white students. Each school-based arrest produces a juvenile or criminal record that affects subsequent employment, housing, and educational opportunities.

**HOM Status (emergent-from-interaction):** These five mechanisms each contribute to the school-to-prison pipeline outcome. The pipeline as a system-level outcome is not produced by any single mechanism and is not intended by any of the architectures involved. No law creates the school-to-prison pipeline; multiple laws and institutional architectures combine to produce it as an emergent interaction effect. Evidence does not rank these mechanisms by their relative contribution to the pipeline — each could be primary; the interaction matters; no evidence resolves the ranking question at Phase 1. **MAXIMAL HOLD-OPEN: the magnitude of the school-to-prison pipeline in PA-3 (how many students; what proportion of Black male SDP students; what trajectory outcomes) is not established by evidence that SD7 can source at Phase 1 [F11-SD7-04; F11-SD7-05]. Closure by analytical assertion would be especially distorting.**

**This is the project's first emergent-from-interaction HOM at full Phase 1 primary engagement.** The shape distinction from commitment-vs-outcome HOM (G7-SD1-03 + D8-Q2 + D10-Q1 + D24-Q1 + G11-SD1-04) is maintained: those five instances involve specific anchor accountability through discrete mechanisms with documented commitment language and measurable outcome gaps. MC48 involves a system-level outcome that no architecture explicitly produces — the phenomenological difference between a gap in fulfilling a specific commitment and an outcome that emerges from interaction.

### Five Own-Territory Threads at SD7

**Thread 1 — School-specific regulatory overlays on regular zoning/building/facilities regimes.** PA School Code provisions specific to schools (occupancy; evacuation planning; safe-school zones; school-specific building requirements beyond the International Building Code baseline) create a regulatory layer on top of the Philadelphia Code provisions that apply to all buildings. D7 and D13 own the regular zoning and building-code architecture; SD7 owns the school-specific overlay. *F11-SD7-06: current SDP school safety zone boundaries; any active school-specific zoning or building-code provisions affecting PA-3 school siting or operation.*

**Thread 2 — Institutional integration architecture.** SDP operates several offices that are the operational-integration hubs: Office of School Safety (SRO coordination; PBIS administration; threat assessment); Office of Student Support Services (social-emotional learning; school counselor coordination; McKinney-Vento liaison; IDEA procedural compliance support); Office of Multilingual Curriculum and Programs (ELL services coordination). These offices are the institutional points where the cross-domain operational integration is managed at the school-system level.

**Thread 3 — School-as-eligibility-gate architecture.** The school is the eligibility-verification hub for multiple assistance programs: immunization compliance gate (D2); CEP-SNAP free-meal eligibility (D12); McKinney-Vento Title VII identification (SD4); IDEA child-find (SD4/SD1); suspension-status affecting juvenile justice referral (MC48); LEP identification (SD4); Free Lunch (D12). Each eligibility gate is individually owned by a cross-domain substantive home; the integrated function — the school as the administrative locus of multiple parallel eligibility determinations — is D11-owned.

**Thread 4 — Educational data architecture.** PIMS (state); SDP ORE (local); CRDC (federal); School Progress Reports — the data architecture is the information-infrastructure layer through which regulatory compliance is demonstrated, program effectiveness is measured, and resource allocation is justified. The commercial ed-tech platform layer (D14/D26 cross-reference) introduces student data privacy complexity.

**Thread 5 — School-day operations integration.** The integrated school day — in which a student might encounter a Title I-funded reading intervention (SD1); a school counselor addressing a behavioral concern (D3 cross-reference); a SRO during a security check; a free lunch via CEP (D12); and an after-school program funded by 21st Century Community Learning Centers grants — is the constituent-lived-experience integration of multiple domain interactions. No single SD captures this integrated experience; SD7 is the synthesis point.

## Section 6: Conversational Narrative Note

SD7 is where D11-Thread C's cross-mechanism synthesis-preview becomes most visible. **Element 3 — Account for the gap.** Three structural mechanisms: (a) MC48 emergent-HOM produces school-to-prison outcomes through mechanism interaction no individual architecture can fully address independently (G11-SD7-01); (b) school-as-eligibility-gate operational integration depends on staff capacity that is constrained by the same adequacy-gap fiscal architecture documented at SD1 (G11-SD7-03); (c) AHERA compliance and school-based mental health staffing both face the specific fiscal-constraint + federal-obligation mismatch pattern (G11-SD7-04; G11-SD7-05) that makes formal provision and actual operational receipt structurally decoupled.

**Element 4 — Meaning for representation.** D11-Thread A at SD7: federal House representation's most direct operational-integration leverage points: AHERA enforcement engagement (USAO-EDPA DPA context); ESSER replacement-funding advocacy (school-based mental health; cross-reference D3); Title I and IDEA funding that enables school-operational capacity; McKinney-Vento Title VII appropriation; and the DOL SRO non-discrimination enforcement architecture. D11-Thread C at SD7: the cross-mechanism synthesis-preview observation — effective representation requires engaging the interaction architecture, not only the component parts. The MC48 emergent-HOM finding operationalizes Thread C at its most analytically demanding: the pipeline is an emergent outcome, and addressing it requires a multi-mechanism response architecture that no single federal program provides.
