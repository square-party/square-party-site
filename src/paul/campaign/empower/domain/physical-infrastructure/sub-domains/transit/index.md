# Transit & Mobility

<p class="domain-lead">Most workers in PA-3 get to work on a bus. SEPTA — the state-created regional authority that runs the city's transit — carries about 800,000 daily riders, with bus alone the second-most-common commute mode citywide. For riders using mobility devices, only about 46% of SEPTA stations are fully accessible, the second-lowest rate among major U.S. transit systems and the structural gap that <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#ada-title-ii">ADA Title II</a> is supposed to close. The 2024–2026 SEPTA fiscal crisis brought the rest of the architecture into view: a roughly $213 million projected operating deficit, August 2025 cuts to 32 bus routes, a 21.5% fare increase to a $2.90 base fare on September 1, a Philadelphia Court of Common Pleas reversal of the service cuts three days later on a <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#title-vi">Title VI</a> disparate-impact theory, and a $394 million capital-to-operating buffer keeping the system intact through fiscal 2026. The federal layer is comparatively stable — <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#fta-section-5307">FTA Section 5307</a> formula apportionment is a statutory entitlement; ADA Title II and Title VI are federally enforceable — but the state architecture underneath is not: <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#act-89">Act 89</a> of 2013's 2022 transition moved $400 million of dedicated PA Turnpike transit funding into the state General Fund, converting a dedicated revenue stream into an annual budget negotiation. Local Philadelphia leverage on SEPTA runs through the 1% local sales tax allocation and board appointments, not direct authority.</p>

## Legal Architecture

### Constitutional and statutory grounding

The legal framework rests on three federal constitutional foundations: the Commerce Clause (U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3) grounding federal regulation of mobility systems affecting interstate commerce; the Spending Clause (U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 1) authorizing federal financial assistance to state and local transit programs; and the Fourteenth Amendment Equal Protection Clause grounding [Title VI](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#title-vi) service-equity requirements applied to federally funded transit (42 U.S.C. § 2000d). The Americans with Disabilities Act Title II (42 U.S.C. § 12131 et seq.) operates at the intersection of these grounds, requiring public transit accessibility.

Pennsylvania constitutional authority for state participation in transit funding rests on **PA Constitution Article VIII** (Taxation and Finance) — the General Assembly's authority to appropriate state funds — and on the Home Rule provisions of **Article IX**, which establish but also constrain Philadelphia's authority to raise local revenue for SEPTA. The 1% local sales tax allocation to SEPTA (carried forward from the [Finance & Taxation](/paul/campaign/empower/domain/finance-taxation/) analysis) operates within this state-permission framework.

### Federal architecture

The federal public transportation program is authorized under **Chapter 53 of Title 49, U.S.C.**, beginning at 49 U.S.C. § 5301. The Federal Transit Administration (FTA), an operating administration within the U.S. Department of Transportation, is the principal federal agency for PA-3 transit. **FTA Region III** is headquartered in Philadelphia, with jurisdiction over Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland, Virginia, West Virginia, and the District of Columbia. The FTA Region III Regional Administrator is **Theresa "Terry" Garcia Crews**; the FTA national Administrator is **Marcus J. Molinaro**. SEPTA is a direct grant recipient under multiple FTA program authorities.

The primary FTA programs affecting PA-3 transit are:

- **Section 5307** (49 U.S.C. § 5307) — Urbanized Area Formula Program, providing formula-distributed federal operating and capital assistance.
- **Section 5337** (49 U.S.C. § 5337) — State of Good Repair Formula Program for fixed-guideway and high-intensity bus systems.
- **Section 5339** (49 U.S.C. § 5339) — Bus and Bus Facilities Program (formula and competitive sub-allocations).
- **Section 5310** (49 U.S.C. § 5310) — Enhanced Mobility of Seniors and Individuals with Disabilities.

Section 5307 operates as a statutory entitlement: the formula is set by statute, the apportionment to urbanized areas follows mechanically once national appropriations are made, and an urbanized area cannot be excluded from formula apportionment by administrative discretion.

The **Capital Investment Grants Program ([CIG](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#cig))** under Section 5309 (49 U.S.C. § 5309) is the primary federal discretionary program for fixed-guideway expansion projects (New Starts, Small Starts, Core Capacity). The IIJA appropriated $1.6 billion per year from the general fund for CIG and authorized another $3.0 billion per year subject to appropriation. SEPTA's major Section 5309 CIG project is **King of Prussia (KOP) Rail** — the four-mile Norristown High Speed Line extension — which entered Project Development Phase under New Starts following FTA approval in October 2021 (re-confirmed April 2025); SEPTA committed $390 million in its FY23 capital budget and is seeking up to 60% federal CIG share. **Trolley Modernization** is funded through SEPTA capital, state Act 89, IIJA, and FTA pilot grants (e.g., a $460,000 grant for a Route 10 corridor study), with a $714 million Alstom contract for 130 ADA-compliant trolley cars; first delivery 2027, all in service by 2031.

The **Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act ([IIJA](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#iija) / "Bipartisan Infrastructure Law"), P.L. 117-58**, signed November 15, 2021, authorized surface transportation programs for FY2022–2026 and authorized up to $108 billion to support federal public transportation programs, including approximately $91 billion in guaranteed funding. **IIJA authorization expires September 30, 2026.** As of April 8, 2026, no draft text of the next surface transportation reauthorization has been released; USDOT issued a nationwide call for feedback in July 2025; congressional extensions are the expected mechanism if no reauthorization is enacted by October 1, 2026.

The **Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2026 (P.L. 119-75 / H.R. 7148)**, signed February 3, 2026 ending a partial government shutdown, included THUD Act provisions that *transferred* approximately $2.3 billion in unobligated IIJA balances to other surface transportation programs. The transfers moved approximately $879 million from the NEVI Program ($503.8M formula + $300M competitive + $75M Joint Office of Energy and Transportation) and additional funds from the Reduction of Truck Emissions at Port Facilities Program into the Tribal Transportation Program, INFRA, and Reconnecting Communities Program. The Reconnecting Communities Program was funded at $30M — 15% of the $200M obligated for FY2026 in the original IIJA. CRISI rail program received only $7M in new appropriations in FY26 (95% of its $137M total derived from IIJA unobligated balances; 93% reduction from FY2025).

**Federal funding flow for SEPTA operating support.** Congress appropriates IIJA-authorized funds annually → FTA apportions Section 5307 formula funds based on population, density, and ridership data → SEPTA as designated recipient draws down apportioned funds. Operating-use eligibility for large urbanized areas like Philadelphia faces formula limits; capital-use eligibility is broad. The FTA Triennial Review and Title VI Program review provide federal compliance oversight.

**Federal administrative vulnerability characterization.** Section 5307 formula apportionment operates as a high-stability statutory entitlement; administrative discretion to withhold apportionment is essentially nil. Section 5309 CIG operates with significant administrative discretion at every stage. IIJA-specific competitive programs (Mega/INFRA/Rural under MPDG) are highly administratively variable.

### State architecture

Pennsylvania's transit funding architecture is built on two principal acts. **Act 44 of 2007** required the Pennsylvania Turnpike Commission to provide PennDOT with $450 million annually for highways, bridges, and public transit. The Act's central funding mechanism — converting Interstate 80 to a tolled highway — was rejected by the Federal Highway Administration in September 2008 (after a second rejection), but the PA Turnpike's annual payment obligation remained in place. **[Act 89](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#act-89) of 2013**, signed by Governor Tom Corbett, restructured the architecture: dedicated the full $450 million annual PA Turnpike payment to public transit, eliminated the $0.12/gallon state retail gas and diesel tax while raising the cap on the Oil Company Franchise Tax, and established a multi-modal fund.

**The 2022 transition.** Act 89 contained a built-in transition that fundamentally altered the funding architecture beginning in fiscal 2022. The PA Turnpike Commission's annual payment to PennDOT was reduced from $450 million to $50 million in 2022, with the $400 million difference shifting to the state's General Fund. This converted the dedicated Turnpike-toll-funded transit subsidy into an annual General Fund appropriation, creating a structural vulnerability that did not previously exist: General Fund appropriations are subject to annual budget negotiations in a way that statutory dedicated revenue streams are not.

**Pennsylvania's transit-specific structural posture.** Pennsylvania remains among the most generous state transit funders on a per-capita basis, but the Act 89 transition has created a structural state-match vulnerability that other states with dedicated revenue streams do not face. The 2024–2026 SEPTA fiscal crisis is an artifact of PA's transit-funding architecture, not of national-level transit funding decline. SEPTA is also unusual nationally in operating all five major terrestrial transit modes (bus, trolleybus, light rail, rapid transit, regional rail) under a single authority — making federal program eligibility analysis more complex than for single-mode systems.

### Local architecture

**SEPTA** (Southeastern Pennsylvania Transportation Authority), created by the Pennsylvania General Assembly on August 17, 1963, commenced operations on February 18, 1964. SEPTA serves five counties — Philadelphia, Delaware, Montgomery, Bucks, and Chester — with the majority of board members appointed by those counties. SEPTA is therefore not a Philadelphia city agency; it is a state-created regional authority with city representation on its board. SEPTA serves approximately 800,000 daily riders, including 52,000 students in the School District of Philadelphia. **SEPTA General Manager: Scott A. Sauer** ("interim" removed from title June 2, 2025).

**The Philadelphia 1% sales tax allocation to SEPTA** (carried forward from the [Finance & Taxation](/paul/campaign/empower/domain/finance-taxation/) analysis) is the local revenue mechanism through which Philadelphia directly funds SEPTA operations.

**SEPTA's 2024–2026 fiscal crisis — staged implementation, court reversal, and capital buffer.** SEPTA announced a structural operating funding gap in 2024, projecting service cuts of approximately 45% beginning in mid-2025 absent additional state funding.

**Federal funding flow at the local level.** SEPTA receives FTA Section 5307 formula funds directly from FTA Region III. PennDOT acts as the state-pass-through entity for state Act 89 dedicated funding. Philadelphia provides the 1% local sales tax allocation. SEPTA fare revenue covers a portion of operating expenses (well below 50% systemwide; typical for major U.S. transit systems).

**Local agencies and offices.** **Philadelphia Office of Transportation, Infrastructure, and Sustainability (OTIS)** coordinates city transportation policy across departments. **Philadelphia Streets Department** (Streets Commissioner: **Kristin Del Rossi**) has jurisdiction over local streets used by SEPTA buses. **Philadelphia City Council Streets & Services Committee** has primary Council jurisdiction over transit-related city issues.

**Home Rule authority and limits.** Philadelphia's Home Rule Charter authorizes the city to enter intergovernmental agreements with SEPTA and to allocate the 1% local sales tax. The charter does not authorize the city to unilaterally modify SEPTA service levels or fare policy — those decisions rest with SEPTA's board. Philadelphia's leverage on SEPTA is exercised through board appointments, intergovernmental coordination, and budget negotiations, not through direct regulatory authority.

## Geography & representation

**Data provenance.** Statistical and structural claims in this section draw on Tier 1 ACS commute mode share data for Philadelphia County (current 2023 ACS 1-year and 5-year estimates available through Census Bureau and OpenDataPhilly's API access); Tier 2 [DVRPC](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#dvrpc) Tracking Progress dashboard tracking Connections 2050 indicators including commute mode share, SEPTA service equity analysis (Title VI Program), OpenDataPhilly transit access mapping, and DVRPC's Equity Through Access (ETA) Map Toolkit and Priority Score; and the chronology of fare and service actions documented above. ZIP-level fare burden / income / vehicle availability would be drawn from ACS 5-year estimates by ZIP — not retrieved at that level for the current analysis.

**PA-3 statistical profile.** Philadelphia County is densely populated with an extensive transit network. Approximately 6 in 10 Philadelphia workers commute by car. About 18% of Philadelphia workers commute by bus — the second-most-common transportation mode. Walking, bicycling, regional rail, subway/elevated, and trolley account for the remainder. These figures contrast sharply with workers living in the Philadelphia suburbs, who overwhelmingly drive (87%) and rarely use buses (2%). Philadelphia is the 5th-highest U.S. metropolitan area for non-car commuters at 28%. The bus-mode share around 18% citywide (25% in some referenced data points) is among the highest in the United States, reflecting structural transit dependence of a substantial portion of the Philadelphia workforce.

SEPTA serves the Philadelphia urbanized area as the designated FTA Section 5307 recipient. SEPTA paid passenger trips reached approximately 174 million in FY23 (an 18% year-over-year increase reflecting partial post-pandemic recovery). SEPTA's structural operating deficit reached approximately $213 million in FY26 (reduced from $240 million after $30 million-plus in austerity savings, including a 7.5% fare increase prior to the current 21.5% increase, hiring freeze, and consultant cuts). Bus accessibility station compliance is approximately 46% — second-lowest among major U.S. transit systems, behind only New York City MTA.

The June 2025 SEPTA board action and its subsequent staged implementation, court reversal, and capital-to-operating buffer (documented in detail under Local Architecture above) represent the most consequential transit service action affecting PA-3 residents in the period covered by this analysis. Federal Title VI compliance requires SEPTA to conduct service-equity analysis when major service changes are proposed; the June 2025 action was the type of major service change that triggers Title VI fare equity analysis and service equity analysis under FTA Circular 4702.1B.

**Geographic variation.** Wage tax rates and federal program eligibility apply uniformly, but transit access and burden vary substantially across PA-3 sub-areas:

- **North/Northwest Philadelphia Core** (Allegheny West, Brewerytown, Cecil B. Moore, Nicetown-Tioga, North Philadelphia West, Sharswood, Strawberry Mansion, Francisville, Fairmount, Spring Garden). Bus and Metro (Broad Street Line for North; Market-Frankford Line via 30th Street, Spring Garden, etc.) provide dense transit access. Bus mode share is highest in this sub-area; transit-dependent populations are concentrated here. Bus route eliminations imposed differential burden on bus-dependent residents.
- **West Philadelphia Core** (Angora, Belmont Village, Carroll Park, Cedar Park, Cobbs Creek, Haddington, Kingsessing, Mantua, Mill Creek, Overbrook Park, Powelton Village, Spruce Hill, University City, Walnut Hill, West Philadelphia, West Powelton, Wynnefield Heights). Trolley routes (T-lines, formerly Routes 10, 11, 13, 34, 36) provide distinct modal access. The Market-Frankford Line provides east-west service. Bus mode share is high. Anchor institution access (Penn, Drexel, CHOP) is internal to the sub-area; the differential is what kind of access is available to residents versus what is available to anchor-institution employees.
- **Northwest Philadelphia** (Andorra, Cedarbrook, Chestnut Hill, East Falls, East Germantown, Germantown, Manayunk, Mt. Airy, Roxborough, Shawmont Valley, Stenton, West Oak Lane, Wissahickon, Wister). The Regional Rail Manayunk/Norristown Line and Chestnut Hill West / Chestnut Hill East lines serve this sub-area, providing commuter rail to Center City. Bus mode share is lower than in the Cores; auto mode share is higher; rail mode share is differentially elevated relative to other sub-areas. The averted January 2026 cuts to five Regional Rail lines would have had differential impact on Northwest Philadelphia commuters who depend on Regional Rail; the averting of those cuts preserved the existing pattern. Regional Rail fare is generally higher than bus/Metro fare; the fare-burden differential affects this sub-area differently than the Cores.
- **South/Southwest Philadelphia** (Devil's Pocket, Dickinson Narrows, Filter Square, Forgotten Bottom, Grays Ferry, Lower Moyamensing, Newbold, Passyunk Square, Point Breeze, Queen Village, Schuylkill, South Philadelphia, West Passyunk, Whitman). The Broad Street Line provides north-south service through South Philadelphia, terminating at NRG Station. The southern terminus is the principal access point for Sports Complex events and for Navy Yard employment. The Broad Street Line does not reach Southwest Philadelphia; bus dependence is correspondingly higher in Southwest. Bicycle commuting ranks second in two South Philadelphia tracts — a sub-area-specific mode pattern not present elsewhere in PA-3. Eastwick / Southwest Philadelphia faces airport adjacency questions: SEPTA Airport Line Regional Rail serves PHL Airport but does not serve as commuter access for most Eastwick/Southwest residents.

The geographic variation pattern across the four sub-areas is characterized less by access-or-no-access than by what kind of transit access is available, what fare structure applies to it, and what reliability profile attends each modal option. The structural representation question for this sub-domain is whether federal House representation engages this differential-by-sub-area pattern, or treats "transit access" as a binary that obscures sub-area variation.

**Pathway tracing.** Three aggregate pathways describe how the architecture is encountered by structurally distinct populations.

*Pathway 1 — Transit-dependent low-wage worker.* A worker without regular access to a personal vehicle, employed in a service-sector or healthcare-support job, commuting from a North/Northwest Core or West Core residence to a downtown or anchor-institution worksite. Monthly fare expenditure (post-September 2025: $2.90 per trip, or monthly TransPass at corresponding tier) consumes a significant portion of monthly take-home pay. Service reliability affects job retention — a SEPTA bus delay can mean job-loss-level consequences for workers whose employers do not tolerate tardiness. The August 2025 service cuts directly exposed this population: workers whose previously-used routes were among the 32 eliminated routes faced either longer combined trips with transfers or paid ride-share alternatives they could not afford. The September 14 service restoration removed the immediate exposure, but the fare increase persisted. Title VI fare equity analysis is the formal protection for this population; whether the analysis was sufficient to flag the disparate impact of the June 2025 action is a question for SEPTA's Title VI documentation review (the underlying disparate-impact theory is what drove the September 4 court order). Federal Section 5307 formula apportionment continues regardless of state-level support fluctuations; the worker's transit access ultimately depends on whether SEPTA's operating budget can sustain service at the level federal capital and operating funding can support.

*Pathway 2 — Senior or disability-status rider.* A rider age 65 or older, or a rider with documented disability, eligible for SEPTA's reduced-fare programs and (where eligible) for paratransit (CCT Connect or "Shared Ride Program"). ADA Title II requires SEPTA to provide complementary paratransit service in the area within ¾ mile of fixed-route service (49 CFR Part 37 implementing regulations). Eligibility determination requires functional assessment; the application process can be a barrier. Once eligible, paratransit service quality (on-time performance, ride duration, scheduling availability) determines effective access. Bus accessibility for fixed-route use depends on the 46% station-accessibility rate — meaning a majority of SEPTA stations are not fully accessible to riders using mobility devices. Senior fare programs (free or reduced fare) operate under state authorization (Pennsylvania's senior transit subsidy is a state program, federal pass-through). The June 2025 fare action's impact on this population is mediated through state senior fare programs; for non-eligible disability riders the fare impact is direct. ADA Title II accessibility compliance is the formal protection; the gap between formal protection and 46% station accessibility is the structural mismatch this pathway encounters.

*Pathway 3 — Cross-jurisdictional commuter.* A worker living in PA-3 (Philadelphia) and employed in a suburban job site (Bucks, Chester, Delaware, Montgomery counties), commuting via Regional Rail. The SEPTA Regional Rail system was historically structured as commuter rail bringing suburban residents to Center City employment; the reverse-commute pattern is structurally less well-served. Service frequency on the reverse-commute pattern is typically lower than on the inbound pattern. Fare structure is distance-based (zone-based), making Philadelphia-to-suburban Regional Rail trips relatively expensive on a per-trip basis. The five-Regional-Rail-line elimination contemplated for January 1, 2026 — if implemented — would have severely disrupted this pathway for affected lines (Paoli/Thorndale serving Main Line employment; Trenton; Wilmington/Newark serving Delaware corridor; Chestnut Hill West; Cynwyd). The averting of those cuts preserved access; the residual fare-burden and service-frequency questions remain. Federal Section 5337 State of Good Repair funding supports SEPTA's Regional Rail infrastructure; federal Section 5307 supports operations.

**Representation question.** Federal law provides Title VI service equity and fare equity protections for federally funded transit, ADA Title II accessibility requirements, and substantial program funding through FTA Sections 5307, 5337, 5339, 5310, and 5309. Pennsylvania law commits substantial state funding to public transportation. Philadelphia Home Rule authorizes the 1% local sales tax allocation. SEPTA itself, as a state-created authority with a five-county board, provides service across PA-3 and the surrounding counties. What PA-3 residents actually experience is service that varies by sub-area, time of day, and modal option: the June 2025 board action directly affected service availability and cost; bus route eliminations imposed differential burden on bus-dependent populations concentrated in the Cores; Regional Rail reverse-commute frequency reductions imposed differential burden on Northwest Philadelphia and on cross-jurisdictional commuters; the 21.5% fare increase persists across all modes; paratransit availability varies; ADA Title II's accessibility floor produces 46% station compliance; fare burden as a share of household income varies by sub-area in inverse relation to income.

The mismatch is structural, not incidental. SEPTA is a state-created regional authority — Philadelphia's leverage over SEPTA's policy is exercised through board appointments and intergovernmental coordination, not direct authority. Pennsylvania's Act 89 transit funding architecture, which moved the $400 million dedicated PA Turnpike payment from the dedicated stream to the General Fund beginning in fiscal 2022, created a structural state-match vulnerability that did not previously exist. When state operating support is squeezed (as in 2024–2026), SEPTA's structural fiscal crisis surfaces — and the response options (fare increases, service cuts) push the burden onto the populations most dependent on transit. The 2025–26 Pennsylvania budget signed November 12, 2025 (Day 135 of impasse) included no new transit funding; SEPTA's near-term operating capacity now depends on the $394 million capital-to-operating buffer authorized September 8, 2025 and the additional $219.9 million directed November 24, 2025. Federal Section 5307 formula apportionment is a high-stability statutory entitlement that protects against complete federal collapse but does not substitute for state operating support. The result is an architecture in which federal protections are stable, state support is structurally vulnerable, and local leverage is indirect — a configuration in which PA-3 transit-dependent residents experience the most-acute consequences of the architecture's vulnerabilities.

Federal House representation for PA-3 has direct levers on the federal-architecture portion of the system: IIJA reauthorization advocacy (Sept 30, 2026 expiration), FTA program oversight, Title VI compliance attention, Section 5309 Capital Investment Grant project advancement (KOP Rail for SEPTA). Federal representation has indirect levers on the state-architecture portion through congressional delegation coordination with state legislative actors and through federal-funding conditions that affect state behavior. Federal representation has no direct authority over SEPTA's operational decisions but can use the federal-funding relationship to influence SEPTA's compliance posture. The structural representation question is thus not whether federal representation cares about transit, but whether federal representation engages the institutional architecture at the level where PA-3 residents experience it — bus route reliability, fare burden, station accessibility, paratransit availability — as opposed to the level at which federal program structure is most legible.

<h2 id="gaps">Gap analysis</h2>

**Gap 1 — Title VI fare and service equity adequacy for the June 2025 SEPTA action.** The SEPTA board's June 26, 2025 action (fare increase to $2.90; 50 bus route eliminations announced — 32 implemented in August 2025 first wave, 18 averted in January 2026 second wave; 5 Regional Rail line cuts averted; 21.5% fare increase implemented) is the type of major service change requiring Title VI fare equity analysis and service equity analysis under FTA Circular 4702.1B. The September 4, 2025 court order — issued on a disparate-impact theory — itself indicates that the original Title VI analysis did not adequately capture or did not foreclose the disparate-impact concern. Whether SEPTA's analysis adequately captured disparate impact on bus-dependent populations concentrated in the North/Northwest Core and West Core is structurally important and not yet documented at the analytical level the equity-impact assessment would require. Federal House representation has direct levers on FTA Title VI compliance review.

**Gap 2 — ADA Title II station accessibility deficit.** SEPTA bus accessibility station compliance is approximately 46%, second-lowest among major U.S. transit systems behind New York City MTA. ADA Title II requires accessible transit operations and complementary paratransit; the 46% figure represents a structural gap between formal protection and actual accessibility for fixed-route users with mobility limitations. The federal levers are DOJ Civil Rights Division engagement, FTA accessibility program advocacy, and federal-aid project ADA compliance attention.

**Gap 3 — PA Act 89 transit funding state-match structural vulnerability.** The Act 89 transition that took effect in fiscal 2022 reduced the PA Turnpike Commission's annual contribution to PennDOT for transit from $450 million to $50 million, with the $400 million difference shifting to the General Fund. This converted the dedicated Turnpike-toll-funded transit subsidy into an annual General Fund appropriation, creating a structural state-match vulnerability that did not previously exist and did not exist in this form for other PA infrastructure programs (Motor License Fund constitutional dedication for highways; gaming-revenue dedication for parks via C2P2). The 2024–2026 SEPTA fiscal crisis is an artifact of this architecture; the November 2025 PA budget passing with no new transit funding confirms the political durability of the underlying configuration. Federal House representation has indirect levers through congressional delegation coordination with state legislative actors; reauthorization-period engagement on federal transit funding levels affects state-match calculations.

**Gap 4 — Cross-jurisdictional commute pattern federal-program engagement.** Reverse-commute and intra-regional commute patterns within the SEPTA service area cross PA-3 / adjacent district boundaries (PA-5 Delaware County, PA-4 Montgomery County, PA-1 Bucks County). Federal transit programs operate at the urbanized-area level rather than at the congressional-district level, meaning the formula-funded service flows benefit cross-district commute patterns regardless of whose district members commute through. The structural feature: PA-3 representation engages the cross-jurisdictional commute pattern at the urbanized-area level rather than the district level. The lever is inter-district congressional delegation coordination on regional transit advocacy.

**Gap 5 — Federal operating support capacity below operational need.** Section 5307 formula apportionment includes operating-support eligibility but with substantial restrictions for large urbanized areas (Philadelphia is among the largest). The federal share for capital projects is up to 80%; for operating expenses, up to 50% maximum. SEPTA's structural ~$213 million operating deficit is not directly addressable through federal operating support at IIJA-authorized levels. IIJA reauthorization could address operating-support eligibility, but this would represent a substantive policy change rather than incremental program adjustment.

**Gap 6 — SEPTA CIG project advancement uncertainty.** The major Section 5309 CIG project for SEPTA is **King of Prussia (KOP) Rail**, the four-mile Norristown High Speed Line extension, which has been in the CIG New Starts Project Development Phase since FTA approval in October 2021 (re-confirmed April 2025). SEPTA committed $390 million in its FY23 capital budget toward KOP Rail and seeks up to 60% federal CIG share. **Trolley Modernization** is the larger transformative initiative ($714 million Alstom contract for 130 ADA-compliant trolley cars; first delivery 2027; all in service by 2031) but is funded primarily through SEPTA capital, state Act 89, IIJA, and FTA pilot grants rather than as a single Section 5309 CIG project. CIG operates with significant administrative discretion at every stage of project advancement. CIG project advocacy is a direct federal House representation lever, particularly significant for KOP Rail given its discretionary status.
