# Federal Infrastructure Funding

<p class="domain-lead">Most of what reaches PA-3 residents through the other six physical-infrastructure sub-domains starts as a check from somewhere in Washington. SEPTA's bus runs, PWD's lead-pipe replacements, the Streets Department's road and signal work, the School District's facility funding, Parks &amp; Recreation's land acquisitions, the Housing Authority's redevelopment plans, and the airport's terminal upgrades all draw federal funds through one of three channels — direct recipient, state pass-through, or competitive grant. The <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#iija">IIJA</a> (P.L. 117-58, signed November 2021) authorized $1.2 trillion over five years, with $550 billion in new investment beyond regular appropriations and a surface-transportation component of $567 billion for FY2022–2026. <strong>Authorizations expire September 30, 2026</strong> — and as of April 2026, no reauthorization bill has been introduced. Two pieces of 2025–26 legislation reshaped the picture mid-cycle: <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#obbba">OBBBA</a> (July 2025) rescinded unobligated funds across multiple IRA-funded equity, climate, and clean-transportation programs and rescinded the <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#justice40">Justice40</a> equity-targeting framework; P.L. 119-75 (February 2026) restructured roughly $2.3 billion in IIJA funds via transfers and rescissions. With reauthorization decisions over the next ~17 months determining FY27–FY31 funding architecture, IIJA reauthorization is the single most consequential near-term federal lever for PA-3 physical infrastructure.</p>

## Legal Architecture

### Constitutional foundation

The constitutional foundation is the federal **Spending Clause** (U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 1), which authorizes Congress to provide financial assistance to state and local governments subject to federal program requirements. The Fourteenth Amendment Equal Protection Clause grounds [Title VI](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#title-vi) as a cross-cutting equity overlay applied to all federally-funded programs. The Tenth Amendment establishes the structural framework within which conditional federal spending operates — federal programs cannot directly compel state action, but federal funding can be conditioned on state acceptance of federal program requirements (with limits per *South Dakota v. Dole* and *NFIB v. Sebelius*).

The constitutional tension specific to this sub-domain: the Spending Clause produces a layered architecture in which federal authority operates only where federal money flows. Where federal money flows, federal floor obligations attach. Where federal money does not flow (or flows in reduced amounts), federal floor obligations diminish — and state and local capacity must substitute. The architecture's vulnerability to administrative variability emerges precisely at this layered structure: the same legal framework can produce different effective outcomes depending on whether federal money is flowing predictably, slowly, or unpredictably.

### Federal architecture

This sub-domain synthesizes federal infrastructure funding architecture across all six substantive sub-domains. Rather than re-describing each federal statute and program, this section traces the cross-cutting structural features.

**[IIJA](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#iija) — Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act / Bipartisan Infrastructure Law (P.L. 117-58).** Signed November 15, 2021. $1.2 trillion total; $550 billion in new investment beyond regular appropriations. The IIJA's surface transportation component reauthorized federal highway, transit, and rail programs for FY2022–2026, providing $567 billion in Highway Trust Fund and General Fund resources to the U.S. Department of Transportation. **Authorizations expire September 30, 2026.** As of January 31, 2026, $568 billion had been allocated to about 68,000 projects nationwide and $275 billion obligated.

**FY 2026 IIJA mechanism (P.L. 119-75 transfers).** The FY 2026 spending legislation (P.L. 119-75, signed February 3, 2026, ending the federal government shutdown) restructured IIJA appropriations through a combination of transfers and rescissions totaling approximately $2.3 billion in IIJA-tied funds. Approximately $879 million was rescinded from NEVI (the National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure Formula Program) and additional funds from the EPA Truck Emissions program; transfers moved funds into the Tribal Transportation Program, INFRA (Nationally Significant Multimodal Freight & Highway Projects), and Reconnecting Communities. Reconnecting Communities was funded at $30 million — approximately 15% of its prior-cycle appropriation level. The mechanism is therefore better characterized as transfers across IIJA program categories than as pure rescissions, though the practical effect on rescinded programs (NEVI, Truck Emissions) is loss of program funding.

**IIJA reauthorization status (April 2026).** No reauthorization bill has been introduced. Congressional committees in both chambers have begun preliminary work; House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee Chairman Sam Graves (R-MO) has signaled a "back to basics" approach emphasizing reduced spending, eliminating mandates, and increasing state flexibility. The Senate Environment and Public Works Committee has been preparing a reauthorization framework. Most observers expect short-term extensions rather than new reauthorization by October 1, 2026; the November 2026 mid-term elections compress the political calendar. The Highway Trust Fund chronic shortfall remains the recurring funding-side structural issue.

**[OBBBA](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#obbba) — One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21).** Signed July 4, 2025. OBBBA constitutes a structural reshaping of the federal infrastructure-program horizon enacted between IIJA passage and IIJA expiration. Key provisions affecting Domain 13:

- **$12.5 billion** for FAA air traffic control modernization.
- **Significant rescissions of unobligated IIJA/IRA funds:** Low-Carbon Transportation Materials Program ($1.9B unobligated rescinded); Clean Heavy-Duty Vehicles Program (~$454M unobligated rescinded); Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund (statutory authority repealed); Environmental Justice Block Grants (eliminated); Climate Pollution Reduction Grants (eliminated); Neighborhood Access and Equity Grant Program (rescinded — relevant to PA-3 because this program was the primary IRA-era complement to IIJA Reconnecting Communities for safety and access projects); state and local environmental review capacity-building IRA $100M (repealed).
- **EV tax credits phased out by September 2025; EV charging infrastructure tax credits phased out by mid-2026 (June 2026).**
- **Methane emissions fee repealed for 10-year period.**
- **125% optional fee for expedited NEPA environmental review** (paid by project sponsors to expedite timelines).
- **Renewable energy on public lands: new 3.9% capacity fee** (25% to host state, 25% to host county).
- **Municipal bond tax-exempt status preserved** (the principal debt-financing pathway for Philadelphia capital programs is preserved — significant positive for cities).
- **Low-Income Housing Tax Credit allocations expanded by 12%** for 9% credits.

OBBBA also rescinded unobligated funds for IRA sustainable forestry programs (relevant to urban canopy work in the parks sub-domain), HUD Green and Resilient Retrofit Program, and various NOAA coastal-resilience programs. The cumulative effect: the federal infrastructure-program horizon for IRA-funded equity, climate, and clean-transportation programs was significantly narrowed in mid-cycle, with effects compounding into the IIJA reauthorization period.

**[Justice40](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#justice40) framework status.** Executive Order 14008 (January 2021, establishing Justice40), EO 12898 (1994, federal environmental justice), and EO 14096 (April 2023, revitalizing EJ for all) were all rescinded or revoked between January and February 2025. All ten EPA regional Environmental Justice offices were closed. EJScreen public access was restricted. The $1 billion in IRA EJ grants was a primary target of these actions. Per White House documentation, more than 518 programs across 16 agencies and approximately $613 billion in funding were affected by the Justice40 framework prior to revocation. Per the Urban Institute's November 2025 retrospective: federal agencies did increase investment to disadvantaged counties in dollar terms during the Justice40 period, with magnitudes varying — DOE and EPA increased disadvantaged-county funding faster than overall funding increases; DOT and DOI did not match overall growth pace. The post-Justice40 distributional pattern of remaining IIJA disbursements (and any extension or reauthorization framework) is a structural unknown affecting PA-3.

**[CDBG](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#cdbg) — Community Development Block Grant.** 42 U.S.C. § 5301 et seq. (Housing and Community Development Act of 1974). Administered by HUD. CDBG is a long-stable program; the Trump administration proposed elimination in the FY 2026 budget request; Congress rejected the elimination and maintained CDBG at $3.3 billion nationally for FY 2026. HOME Investment Partnerships at $1.25 billion, Continuum of Care at $4.1 billion, Choice Neighborhoods at $350 million. Philadelphia is an entitlement grantee receiving annual formula allocation. CDBG funds are eligible for "public facilities and improvements" — usable for streetscape, parks, public buildings (with school exceptions). Philadelphia's CDBG-DR allocation in response to Hurricane Ida totaled $163,204,000 (per Federal Register notices May 24, 2022 and January 23, 2023); Philadelphia announced CDBG-DR Action Plan Amendment #3 on March 12, 2026.

**[Stafford Act](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#stafford-act) FEMA programs.** 42 U.S.C. § 5121 et seq. Public Assistance for declared disasters; Hazard Mitigation Grant Program ([HMGP](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#hmgp), Section 404); Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities ([BRIC](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#bric), IIJA-funded). FEMA Region III (Philadelphia) administers these in PA-3.

**EPA infrastructure programs.** SDWA SRF ([DWSRF](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#dwsrf-cwsrf)), CWA SRF ([CWSRF](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#dwsrf-cwsrf)) (administered through [PENNVEST](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#pennvest) in Pennsylvania); WIIN grants; WIFIA loans; Brownfields program; Clean School Bus Program; various other IIJA-specific authorities.

**HUD infrastructure-relevant programs.** CDBG (described above); HOME (housing-oriented but with infrastructure interactions); Section 108 loan guarantees; Choice Neighborhoods. The Philadelphia Housing Authority (PHA) — an MTW agency since April 2001 — operates the "Opening Doors" asset repositioning plan with estimated cost growing from $3.8 billion (FY 2026 plan) to $6.8 billion (FY 2027 plan); approximately 78% of Philadelphia housing organization funding comes from federal sources (per Reinvestment Fund/Pew analysis FY 2021–2023).

**Federal agencies in Philadelphia.** Multiple federal agency Region offices are headquartered in Philadelphia, providing relatively easy access for PA-3 advocates and city/SDP/PWD staff:

- EPA Region III (1650 Arch Street)
- FTA Region III (1760 Market Street; FTA Administrator Marcus J. Molinaro; FTA Region III Regional Administrator Theresa "Terry" Garcia Crews)
- FEMA Region III
- HUD Mid-Atlantic Field Office
- USACE Philadelphia District
- FRA, FAA Eastern Region

FHWA Pennsylvania Division Office is in Harrisburg, not Philadelphia. The geographic concentration of federal Region III offices in Philadelphia is itself an institutional asset for PA-3 — federal agency engagement is logistically easier for Philadelphia-based actors than for actors in regions where Region offices are more distant.

### State architecture

**Pennsylvania pass-through agencies for federal infrastructure funds:**

- **PennDOT** — primary recipient for FHWA federal-aid highway funds; programs through STIP and (for metropolitan areas) DVRPC TIP. PennDOT Secretary Mike Carroll.
- **PA DEP** — primary state agency for EPA SRF capitalization (which then flows through PENNVEST as state authority).
- **PENNVEST** — DWSRF and CWSRF administrator; provides loans and grants to municipal water systems including PWD.
- **PA DCNR** — LWCF state liaison agency; administers state-side LWCF grants and the C2P2 state program.
- **PDE** — federal Title I, IDEA, and other USED operating funds; PlanCon (in moratorium); recent Public School Facility Improvement Grant Program.

**PA state transit funding (FY 2026–27).** Governor Shapiro's FY 2026–27 budget proposal (announced February 19, 2026) includes transferring an additional 1.75% of Pennsylvania Sales and Use Tax revenues to the Pennsylvania Transportation Trust Fund beginning July 1, 2027 — providing approximately $300–319.6 million annually in recurring transit funding statewide. The FY 2026–27 budget has not been enacted as of April 27, 2026; the deadline is June 30, 2026. The Republican-controlled state Senate has previously declined to advance Shapiro's recurring-transit-funding proposal; the structural impasse on long-term recurring transit funding remains unresolved despite the cumulative emergency interventions documented in the Transit & Mobility analysis (the November 2024 $153M federal highway flex; the 2024–25 budget $80M; the September 2025 $394M SEPTA capital→operating; the November 2025 ~$220M for Silverliner IV).

**State matching obligations.** Federal infrastructure programs typically require state and local matching funds (often 80% federal / 20% state-or-local for highway and transit; 50/50 or other ratios for other programs). State match obligations affect Pennsylvania's ability to access full federal apportionments — match shortfalls cause federal funds to remain undrawn or get redistributed to other states.

**Pennsylvania-specific features.** PA's Constitutional dedication of Motor License Fund revenue to highway purposes (Article VIII § 11) supports the state-match capacity for federal-aid highway programs. PA's gaming revenue dedication to C2P2 provides state-match capacity for parks programs that other states lack. PA's lack of a dedicated transit revenue stream (post-[Act 89](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#act-89) transition shifted Turnpike payment to General Fund) creates a structural state-match vulnerability for federal transit programs that does not exist for highway or parks programs. This is the structural state-architecture context for SEPTA's recurring fiscal vulnerability documented in the Transit & Mobility sub-domain.

### Local architecture

**Philadelphia as direct federal recipient.** Multiple Philadelphia-area entities receive federal infrastructure funds directly (rather than through state pass-through):

- **SEPTA** receives FTA grants directly as designated recipient for the urbanized area.
- **City of Philadelphia (multiple departments)** receives CDBG entitlement directly from HUD.
- **PWD** receives some EPA grants directly, in addition to PENNVEST pass-through.
- **PHA** receives HUD program funds as a Moving to Work agency.
- **SDP** receives some federal education program funds directly through PDE pass-through and from USED.
- **Philadelphia International Airport** receives FAA AIP and IIJA Airport Terminal Program funding (~$375 million in IIJA Airport Terminal Program funding since 2021).

**Direct-recipient status is structurally consequential** because it reduces dependence on state-level prioritization and political negotiation — SEPTA does not need PennDOT permission to draw down its FTA Section 5307 apportionment; CDBG flows to Philadelphia regardless of state legislative action. This direct-recipient structure provides federal funding stability for Philadelphia even when state-level support fluctuates — though as the SEPTA fiscal crisis demonstrates, direct-recipient status does not insulate against state-architecture vulnerabilities for programs with significant operating-funding requirements.

**Philadelphia CDBG.** As an entitlement grantee, Philadelphia receives an annual CDBG allocation based on a HUD formula (population, poverty, age of housing). Philadelphia's CDBG allocation is among the larger entitlement allocations nationally, reflecting the city's scale and need profile. Physical infrastructure is one eligible use category among many; Philadelphia's CDBG plan determines the share allocated to physical infrastructure each year. Philadelphia's CDBG-DR Hurricane Ida allocation totals $163.2 million and is administered by the City Office of the Director of Finance.

**Federal funding flow synthesis.** Federal infrastructure funds reach PA-3 residents through multiple parallel channels:

- **Direct federal-recipient channel:** SEPTA, PWD, City CDBG, SDP federal operating, PHA HUD funds, Philadelphia International Airport.
- **State-pass-through channel:** PennDOT (FHWA → state-route projects); PA DEP/PENNVEST (EPA → PWD); DCNR ([LWCF](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#lwcf) → PPR).
- **Hybrid channel:** Some programs combine federal allocation with state administration with local execution (HSIP for highways; some EPA programs).

The multi-channel architecture is, in formal terms, an institutional asset — multiple paths exist for federal funds to reach PA-3 residents. In practice, the multi-channel architecture creates coordination challenges: each channel has different reporting requirements, match expectations, and timing. Smaller PA-3 institutional actors (community organizations, smaller nonprofits) often cannot navigate the full multi-channel architecture and must rely on the larger institutional actors (SEPTA, PWD, SDP, City, PPR, PHA) to access federal resources.

### Administrative-vulnerability taxonomy applied to physical-infrastructure programs

Per the Finance & Taxation analysis's administrative-vulnerability taxonomy, the following preliminary categorization applies to Domain 13 physical-infrastructure federal programs. The categories: **ROBUST** (statutory entitlement, formula-based, high-stability); **OPERATIVE** (regular but containing some discretionary elements); **ADMINISTRATIVELY VARIABLE** (significant discretionary variation in administration); **APPROPRIATIONS-DEPENDENT** (subject to annual appropriation outcomes); **SPARSE** (highly discretionary or cooperative-agreement-based with limited stability).

**ROBUST (statutory entitlement, formula-based):**

- FTA Section 5307 Urbanized Area Formula
- FTA Section 5337 State of Good Repair Formula
- FHWA NHPP, STBG, HSIP formula apportionments
- CDBG entitlement formula
- LWCF state-side program (post-Great American Outdoors Act permanent appropriation; protected against the FY 2026 admin diversion proposal by Congressional Appropriations Subcommittee action)
- DWSRF/CWSRF state allocation formulas

**OPERATIVE (regular but with discretionary elements):**

- EPA SDWA primacy oversight (state primacy holds; EPA enforcement discretionary)
- FHWA project-level oversight (formula funds are ROBUST but project advancement involves discretion)
- PENNVEST loan award decisions (within DWSRF/CWSRF formula state allocation)

**ADMINISTRATIVELY VARIABLE:**

- FTA Section 5309 Capital Investment Grants ([CIG](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#cig)) — multi-stage administrative review (SEPTA KOP Rail is the major PA-3 CIG project in Project Development Phase; $390M FY23 SEPTA capital; 60% federal share sought)
- IIJA-specific competitive programs (MEGA, INFRA, Rural under MPDG; BRIC)
- Outdoor Recreation Legacy Partnership (LWCF competitive subset)
- EPA Brownfields competitive grants
- Stafford Act Hazard Mitigation discretionary subsets

**APPROPRIATIONS-DEPENDENT:**

- NEVI (~$879M rescinded in FY 2026 P.L. 119-75 transfer mechanism — illustrative of the appropriations-dependent vulnerability category)
- Some IIJA discretionary programs subject to annual appropriations
- AHERA enforcement funding (federal monitoring is not a federal-grant flow)
- SASI (technical assistance) program
- IRA-funded programs subject to OBBBA rescissions (IRA Forest Service sustainable forestry; IRA EJ Block Grants; Climate Pollution Reduction Grants; Neighborhood Access and Equity)

**SPARSE:**

- Specific cooperative-agreement-based programs with limited Philadelphia application
- Some pilot programs with uncertain re-authorization

### IIJA reauthorization as structural inflection point

The September 30, 2026 IIJA expiration is the most consequential near-term variable for PA-3 physical infrastructure funding. Three scenarios:

1. **Reauthorization at or above IIJA levels.** Formula programs continue. Competitive program funding stable or increased. The core funding architecture is preserved.
2. **Reauthorization below IIJA levels.** Formula programs revert to lower (pre-IIJA) baselines. The $550 billion in new investment beyond regular appropriations falls away. Highway, bridge, transit, water programs all face funding reductions.
3. **No reauthorization (extension or lapse).** Formula programs continue at pre-IIJA levels via continuing-resolution mechanisms. New competitive program awards halt. Long-term authorization uncertainty creates planning chill effects across multiple PA-3 capital programs.

As of April 2026, no reauthorization bill has been introduced; most observers expect short-term extensions rather than full reauthorization by October 1, 2026.

## Geography & representation

**Data provenance.** Statistical and structural claims in this section draw on FTA, FHWA, EPA, FEMA, HUD, and DVRPC programmatic data; IIJA implementation tracking from DVRPC's nine-county summary through December 2023; SEPTA capital and operating documentation; PENNVEST award records; CDBG-DR Federal Register notices; and the chronology of FY2025–FY2026 statutory and administrative changes (OBBBA July 2025; Justice40 revocation January–February 2025; P.L. 119-75 February 2026; PA budget November 2025).

**IIJA scale and expiration timeline.** IIJA (P.L. 117-58, signed November 15, 2021) authorized $1.2 trillion in spending over five years, with $550 billion in new investment beyond regular appropriations. Surface transportation component: $567 billion in HTF and General Fund resources. Authorizations expire September 30, 2026. As of January 31, 2026, $568 billion allocated to ~68,000 projects nationwide; $275 billion obligated. Approximately $492 billion remained to be allocated for use between 2024 and 2026 as of early 2024.

**Pennsylvania state-level IIJA scale.** PennDOT receives over $4 billion in additional highway and bridge funds over five years from IIJA, plus competitive grant awards. PA DEP receives approximately $400 million over 10 years for orphan and abandoned oil and gas wells. PENNVEST receives substantial DWSRF/CWSRF capitalization including the IIJA-specific lead service line replacement set-asides. PA DCNR receives LWCF state-side allocations.

**Greater Philadelphia / PA-3 IIJA scale.** [DVRPC](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#dvrpc) tracked over $458 million in IIJA competitive grants to the nine-county region through December 2023. Major individual awards include the SEPTA $317 million Rail Vehicle Replacement Program (February 2024). Philadelphia International Airport has received approximately $375 million in IIJA Airport Terminal Program funding since 2021. Specific PA-3 IIJA awards have included the Eastwick Near-term Flood Barrier Project ($1,383,069 secured by Rep Mary Gay Scanlon, PA-5, in October 2024 — cross-jurisdictional).

**FY 2026 P.L. 119-75 transfer/rescission detail.** The FY 2026 spending legislation (P.L. 119-75, signed February 3, 2026) restructured approximately $2.3 billion in IIJA-tied funds via transfers and rescissions. NEVI: ~$879M rescinded. EPA Truck Emissions: significant rescission. Reconnecting Communities: funded at $30M (~15% of prior cycle). Tribal Transportation Program, INFRA: increased through transfers. Beyond the FY26 mechanism, broader OBBBA-attributable rescissions (July 2025) eliminated unobligated funds across multiple IRA programs (see Federal architecture section above).

**Geographic variation by funding channel.** For this sub-domain, geographic variation is best understood as variation in funding-channel distribution rather than as sub-area-level infrastructure variation. The four-sub-area framework's relevance is indirect: PA-3 sub-areas experience the federal funding architecture through institutional intermediaries (SEPTA serves all sub-areas with differential modal mix; PWD serves all sub-areas with differential lead service line concentration; Streets Department / PennDOT serve all sub-areas with differential HIN concentration; and so on). The federal funding architecture's geographic distribution is therefore expressed through institutional flows rather than directly mapped to sub-areas.

- *Direct-recipient channel.* SEPTA, PWD, City CDBG, PHA, SDP federal operating, PPR LWCF state-side awards, and Philadelphia International Airport. The structural feature: these flows are insulated from state-level prioritization but are exposed to federal program design (formula vs. competitive), federal agency administrative practice (review timelines, compliance requirements), and federal appropriations cycles. The 2024–2026 SEPTA service-cut crisis illustrates the structural limitation of direct-recipient federal flows — Section 5307 formula apportionment did not collapse, but state operating support shifts (post–Act 89 transition, plus FY24–FY25 fiscal pressure) produced operational consequences that direct-federal-recipient status did not insulate against.
- *State-pass-through channel.* PennDOT (FHWA federal-aid → state-route projects in PA-3); PENNVEST (DWSRF/CWSRF capitalization → PWD lead service line replacement and CSO-related work); PA DCNR (LWCF state-side → PPR awards plus C2P2 state grants); PDE (Public School Facility Improvement Grant Program plus IIJA cross-cutting program flows → SDP). The structural feature: state-pass-through requires state-level prioritization decisions, state-level matching capacity, and coordination across multiple state agencies.
- *Competitive-grant channel.* Section 5309 CIG (SEPTA KOP Rail is the major PA-3 CIG project in Project Development Phase); IIJA-specific competitive programs (MEGA, INFRA, Rural under MPDG; BRIC; ORLP; CRP discretionary; SMART; ATIIP); EPA Brownfields competitive grants; FEMA Hazard Mitigation discretionary subsets. The structural feature: competitive grants involve administrative discretion at every stage of project advancement, making this channel the most administratively variable. The Justice40 revocation in early 2025 affects competitive-grant priorities specifically — many competitive programs had been scoring criteria favoring disadvantaged-community impact during the Justice40 period; post-revocation criteria are different.
- *Cross-jurisdictional channel.* Specific PA-3 federal funding flows have crossed congressional district boundaries in the period covered by this analysis. The Eastwick HESCO project ($1,383,069, October 2024) was secured by Rep Mary Gay Scanlon (PA-5), reflecting both Cobbs Creek's role as the PA-3/PA-5 boundary and the inter-district coordination required for flood resilience. Similar inter-district dynamics may apply to other PA-3 federal-funding flows where projects affect or originate in adjacent districts (Wissahickon Watershed extending to Montgomery County / PA-4 boundary-adjacent; John Heinz NWR at the Delaware County boundary; transit corridors crossing PA-3/PA-5).

**Domain-synthesis representation question.** The federal infrastructure funding architecture for PA-3 operates through the Spending Clause, conditioned on federal program requirements; the Title VI overlay grounds cross-cutting equity; the Tenth Amendment establishes the federalism framework. IIJA is the operative authorization for surface transportation, drinking water, and wastewater programs, plus cross-cutting infrastructure. OBBBA restructured the IRA-funded portion of the federal infrastructure-program horizon. P.L. 119-75 restructured FY 2026 IIJA appropriations through transfers and rescissions. The domain-specific federal architectures within the substantive sub-domains operate within this Spending Clause framework. State-level architectures overlay the federal floor with state-level requirements. Local Home Rule architectures provide municipal authority within the federal-state framework.

What PA-3 residents experience is the federal funding architecture through institutional intermediaries (SEPTA, PWD, Streets Department/PennDOT, Sanitation Department, SDP, PPR, PHA) and through differential outcomes by sub-area in each substantive sub-domain. The cumulative racial disadvantage geography expresses itself in differential infrastructure burden across lead service lines (water sub-domain), HIN concentration (roads sub-domain), dumping concentration (waste sub-domain), building condition (schools sub-domain), and park-quality and tree-canopy differentials (parks sub-domain). Federal funding flows reach PA-3 through multiple parallel channels with each channel's structural features producing different outcomes. The 2024–2026 period has been one of structural disruption: SEPTA fiscal crisis with August–September 2025 service-cut/court-order/restoration sequence; AHERA DPA filing in June 2025 (operationally in effect by late 2025); William Penn ruling becoming final in July 2023 with $565M allocated in November 2025 PA budget; Eastwick flood resilience work advancing toward implementation; Justice40 revocation in early 2025; OBBBA enactment July 2025 with significant IRA-program rescissions; FY26 P.L. 119-75 transfers/rescissions of $2.3+ billion in IIJA funds (February 2026); IIJA reauthorization expiration approaching September 30, 2026 with no bill introduced as of April 2026.

The mismatch between formal provision and actual experience is structural and operates through identifiable mechanisms. *First*, the layered enforcement architecture — federal floor, state implementation, local operations — produces variable effective protection depending on which layer is operative for a given issue. *Second*, the channel-specific administrative vulnerability — formula programs (ROBUST) operate with high stability; competitive programs (ADMINISTRATIVELY VARIABLE) operate with significant administrative discretion; appropriations-dependent programs face annual budget risk. The FY 2026 P.L. 119-75 transfers/rescissions and the OBBBA-attributable IRA rescissions illustrate that even programs nominally in stable categories can face mid-cycle reductions or program-horizon narrowing. *Third*, the constitutional inadequacy of state funding for some sub-domains — most prominently schools per William Penn — operates as a structural floor below what the Pennsylvania Constitution requires; federal-floor compliance (AHERA) cannot be defended against by state funding inadequacy. *Fourth*, the institutional capacity gaps — for example, PPD Environmental Crimes Unit at 2 staff for citywide illegal dumping enforcement; the absence of federal water-affordability programs of the LIHEAP scale; the gap between AHERA enforcement scope and federal compliance financing — represent structural mismatches between problem scale and institutional response capacity. *Fifth*, the cross-jurisdictional and cross-domain coordination requirements produce representational complexity that no single agency or program addresses comprehensively. *Sixth*, the post-Justice40 federal administrative architecture for equity-targeting has been thinned in ways that affect federal program design across all sub-domains in this domain.

Federal House representation has direct levers on the federal portion of the architecture. The most consequential near-term direct lever is the IIJA reauthorization process (or extension/lapse outcome). Reauthorization decisions during the next ~17 months will affect the funding levels, formula structures, competitive program design, and equity-targeting criteria that determine federal funding flow to PA-3 institutional actors over the FY27–FY31 period. Federal House representation also has direct levers on rescission protection (P.L. 119-75 negotiations involve appropriations committees; future rescissions can be opposed); on inter-district coordination (Eastwick illustrates the value of coordination with PA-5 specifically and adjacent districts generally); on federal agency engagement through the Region III geography (EPA, FTA, FEMA, USACE, HUD Region offices in Philadelphia provide geographic-proximity advantage for project advocacy); and on protection of Great American Outdoors Act mandatory LWCF funding against future diversion proposals. Federal House representation has indirect levers on the state portion of the architecture through congressional delegation coordination and through federal-funding conditions that affect state behavior. Federal House representation has limited direct levers on local Home Rule operations but can use federal-funding relationships to support specific local initiatives.

The synthetic representation question for the domain: does PA-3 federal House representation engage the multi-channel federal-state-local infrastructure funding architecture at the level where PA-3 residents experience the architecture's vulnerabilities, or does federal representation default to the formal-program-design level at which federal architecture is most legible? The level at which PA-3 residents experience the architecture's vulnerabilities is concrete: the bus route eliminated and then restored by court order in the August–September 2025 SEPTA action; the lead service line in a North Philadelphia rowhome facing the LCRR/LCRI notification; the HIN corridor where the family member walked to a bus stop; the dumping hot spot adjacent to a vacant lot; the asbestos-cited classroom; the heat-vulnerable tree-canopy-deficient neighborhood; the flood-vulnerable Eastwick rowhome; the cumulative effect of these across the cumulative racial disadvantage geography. The level at which federal architecture is most legible is the program-by-program structure of FTA, FHWA, EPA, FEMA, HUD, USACE, AHERA, and other federal authorities. The structural representation choice is whether federal representation attempts to bridge these levels — engaging both the formal-architecture-design level (reauthorization, program structure, equity criteria) and the constituent-experience level (specific projects, specific institutional gaps, specific sub-area patterns) — or operates primarily at one level.

<h2 id="gaps">Gap analysis</h2>

**Gap 1 — IIJA reauthorization status with no bill yet introduced.** IIJA expires September 30, 2026. As of April 2026, no reauthorization bill has been introduced. House T&I Chairman Sam Graves (R-MO) signaled "back to basics" approach. Funding-side agreement (Senate Finance and House Ways and Means) is the structural barrier; Highway Trust Fund chronic shortfall is the recurring funding-side issue. November 2026 mid-term elections compress timing. Most observers expect extensions rather than new reauthorization by October 1, 2026. This is the most consequential near-term federal lever for the entire physical-infrastructure domain — reauthorization decisions over the next ~17 months affect FY27–FY31 funding architecture across SEPTA, PWD, Streets, SDP, PPR, PHA, and the airport.

**Gap 2 — Justice40 framework revocation and equity-targeting effects.** EO 14008 (January 2021), EO 12898 (1994), and EO 14096 (April 2023) were all revoked or rescinded January–February 2025. About half of IIJA's ~400 programs were subject to Justice40. EPA's ten regional EJ offices were closed; EJScreen public access was restricted. Per Urban Institute November 2025 retrospective: DOE and EPA exceeded overall growth in disadvantaged-county funding during the Justice40 period; DOT and DOI lagged. Post-revocation distributional patterns are a structural unknown. Federal House representation can advocate for restored or alternative equity-targeting criteria in reauthorization; statutory Title VI floor remains a substantive lever.

**Gap 3 — Mid-cycle rescission risk; OBBBA-attributable program-horizon narrowing.** P.L. 119-75 (February 3, 2026) restructured approximately $2.3 billion in IIJA-tied funds via transfers and rescissions, including ~$879 million from NEVI. OBBBA (P.L. 119-21, July 4, 2025) rescinded unobligated funds across multiple IRA programs (Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund statutory authority repealed; Environmental Justice Block Grants eliminated; Climate Pollution Reduction Grants eliminated; Neighborhood Access and Equity rescinded; IRA sustainable forestry funds rescinded; HUD Green and Resilient Retrofit rescinded). Even nominally stable program categories can face mid-cycle reductions or program-horizon narrowing. Federal House representation engagement with appropriations process; rescission protection as ongoing representational work; defense of LWCF Great American Outdoors Act mandatory funding against single-cycle administrative diversion proposals.

**Gap 4 — Federal-criminal-floor enforcement without federal compliance financing.** AHERA enforcement (the school-buildings sub-domain's structural finding) is the most direct expression of this gap, but the pattern extends to other federal-floor obligations (SDWA without federal water-affordability program; ADA Title II without dedicated federal compliance financing for municipalities; LCRR/LCRI requirements with limited customer-side replacement financing). The structural feature: federal-floor enforcement operates on its own terms; federal financial assistance for compliance is structured separately and often inadequately. Federal House representation can advocate for federal financing-and-enforcement integration in reauthorization and program development.

**Gap 5 — Authorization-to-disbursement gap as structural feature.** The cyclical pattern of major reauthorization providing a multi-year horizon → early-year disbursement → late-year acceleration → political composition shifts producing rescissions/cancellations/revocations is a structural feature of the federal infrastructure funding architecture. The IIJA period since 2021 has made this feature unusually visible due to the IIJA's unprecedented scale; the 2025–2026 period of OBBBA enactment, P.L. 119-75 transfers/rescissions, and Justice40 revocation has compressed the visibility of this pattern. The feature is older and more general than the present moment. Federal House representation engagement requires treating announcements as the beginning of representational work rather than its conclusion; sustained agency-level and appropriations-level engagement protects flows through implementation.

**Gap 6 — Cross-jurisdictional coordination requirements.** Eastwick / Cobbs Creek illustrates that PA-3 federal-funding flows can require inter-district coordination with adjacent congressional districts (PA-5 specifically; PA-4 for Wissahickon Watershed boundary-adjacent dynamics). John Heinz NWR at the Delaware County boundary represents a federal-management cross-jurisdictional dynamic. The current operational expression of inter-district coordination is uneven; the Eastwick example demonstrates effective coordination, but equivalent attention to other potential cross-jurisdictional pathways is not documented. Inter-district congressional delegation coordination is a structural feature of PA-3 representation work.

**Gap 7 — PA-3-specific federal funding aggregate data gap.** DVRPC tracks IIJA awards to the nine-county Greater Philadelphia region; PennDOT tracks state-level IIJA awards; agency-specific records exist. PA-3-specific aggregate federal funding by program for FY24–FY26 was not surfaced at the integrated analytical level the synthesis would optimally support. Federal House representation can develop systematic federal-funding tracking for PA-3 to support engagement with reauthorization, appropriations, and rescission protection.
