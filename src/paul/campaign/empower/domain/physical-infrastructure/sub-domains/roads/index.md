# Roads, Bridges & Pedestrian Infrastructure

<p class="domain-lead">About 12% of Philadelphia's streets account for 80% of the city's killed-and-seriously-injured traffic crashes. The <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#vision-zero">Vision Zero</a> Action Plan 2030 — released November 2025 by OTIS, PDPH, the Streets Department, and OEM, fulfilling Mayor Parker's March 2024 executive order — documents that this <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#hin">High Injury Network</a> concentrates in the same neighborhoods marked by the cumulative racial disadvantage geography of PA-3: 137 of roughly 198 HIN miles run through tracts scoring highest on the <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#pdph-uc-metric">PDPH Underserved Communities</a> metric, and killed-or-seriously-injured rates are 2.4 times higher in those tracts than in the lowest-UC tracts. Pedestrians, cyclists, and motorcyclists make up under 10% of crashes but two-thirds of fatalities; for pedestrians using mobility devices, <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#ada-title-ii">ADA Title II</a> requires accessible sidewalks, curb ramps, and pedestrian signals — but Philadelphia's pre-1940 housing stock concentration corresponds to older sidewalk infrastructure with incremental rather than systematic compliance. Federal Title 23 programs (NHPP, STBG, HSIP, BIP, CRP) flow primarily to state-route projects through PennDOT and to federal-aid metropolitan projects through <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#dvrpc">DVRPC</a> TIP programming; local-street capital is largely city-funded. The Roosevelt Boulevard automated speed enforcement program — in place since 2020 — shows what aligned federal-floor and city operations can do: a 95%+ reduction in speeding violations, 21% reduction in fatal/serious-injury crashes, 50% reduction in pedestrian-involved crashes.</p>

## Legal Architecture

### Constitutional and statutory grounding

Federal authority over roads, bridges, and pedestrian infrastructure rests on the same Commerce Clause and Spending Clause grounds as transit. The Federal-Aid Highway Program operates under Spending Clause authority — federal financial assistance to state highway programs is conditioned on state compliance with federal requirements (design standards, environmental review, [Title VI](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#title-vi)). The Americans with Disabilities Act Title II (42 U.S.C. § 12131 et seq.) is constitutionally grounded in § 5 of the 14th Amendment and obligates public entities to ensure accessibility of sidewalks, pedestrian crossings, transit stops, curb ramps, and other public infrastructure.

Pennsylvania's constitutional authority for state roads rests on **PA Const. art. VIII, § 11** — the Motor License Fund, which constitutionally dedicates motor fuel tax revenue and motor vehicle registration fees to highway and bridge purposes. This dedication is structurally consequential: motor-fuel-tax revenue cannot be diverted to non-highway purposes, which constrains state policy responses to transit funding crises but also protects state highway funding from competing demands. Pennsylvania Home Rule authority (Article IX) authorizes Philadelphia's Streets Department local-jurisdiction authority over local streets, subject to the boundary with state-route authority.

The constitutional tension specific to roads and bridges: the federal floor (Title VI environmental justice review of federal-aid projects; ADA Title II accessibility requirements) interacts with the state-route / local-street jurisdictional split in complex ways. A federal-aid project on a state route within Philadelphia is subject to Title VI review at the project level but is ultimately PennDOT's responsibility for compliance. A non-federal-aid local street project on a Philadelphia city street is subject to Streets Department jurisdiction with much weaker formal Title VI review architecture. The result is differential federal-floor application across the same urban roadway network depending on jurisdictional and funding-source status.

### Federal architecture

**Federal-Aid Highway Program** is codified at Title 23, U.S.C., administered by the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA). Key formula programs: **National Highway Performance Program (NHPP)** at 23 U.S.C. § 119 (Interstate plus principal arterial highways — I-76 Schuylkill Expressway, I-95, I-676 Vine Street Expressway within PA-3); **Surface Transportation Block Grant Program (STBG)** at 23 U.S.C. § 133 (flexible formula funding usable for road, bridge, transit, and bike/pedestrian projects); **Highway Safety Improvement Program (HSIP)** at 23 U.S.C. § 148 (formula funding for safety-focused improvements); **Bridge Investment Program (BIP)** (created under [IIJA](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#iija); competitive grants for bridge replacement and rehabilitation); **Carbon Reduction Program (CRP)** (created under IIJA; formula funding for carbon-reduction projects).

The IIJA reauthorized these programs for FY2022–2026. **The September 30, 2026 IIJA expiration** affects future authorizations. As of April 8, 2026, no draft text for the next surface transportation reauthorization has been released. Continuation of HIN safety funding past September 30, 2026 depends on reauthorization outcome or congressional extension.

**FHWA Pennsylvania Division Office** is located in Harrisburg (the state capital, not Philadelphia). The Division Office provides federal oversight of PennDOT as the primary recipient of federal-aid highway funds.

**ADA Title II.** Public entities must ensure that newly constructed and altered facilities are accessible. The Department of Justice issues Title II regulations; DOJ Civil Rights Division enforces. The U.S. Access Board issues design standards (Public Right-of-Way Accessibility Guidelines, PROWAG, and Accessibility Standards for Buildings and Facilities). Federal-aid highway projects must comply with Title II as well as with ADA Title III standards in some applications.

**Federal funding flow for roads and bridges.** Congress appropriates Federal-Aid Highway Program funds → FHWA apportions formula funds to states → PennDOT receives Pennsylvania's apportionment and programs it through the Statewide Transportation Improvement Program (STIP) → DVRPC programs the federal share of metropolitan-area projects through the Transportation Improvement Program (TIP) → individual projects receive obligation authority and proceed to construction. PennDOT and Philadelphia Streets Department are both federal-aid recipients depending on project type. [CDBG](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#cdbg) can fund streetscape improvements.

### State architecture

**Pennsylvania Department of Transportation (PennDOT) District 6-0** (King of Prussia) administers the state-route network within Philadelphia, including the Interstate system, US Routes (US 1, US 13, US 30), and PA-numbered routes within city limits. The state-route portion of Philadelphia's road network is generally the higher-functional-class roadway network — Interstate freeways, arterial principals — while local streets are Philadelphia Streets Department jurisdiction. The split is jurisdictionally clean but operationally interrelated; coordination between PennDOT and the Streets Department on traffic management, snow operations, and emergency response is recurrent.

**State statutes.** The **Pennsylvania Vehicle Code**, 75 Pa. C.S. § 101 et seq., governs vehicle operations, traffic regulation, and vehicle registration. The **State Highway Law**, 36 P.S. § 670-101 et seq., establishes PennDOT's authority over state-route designation and maintenance. **[Act 89](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#act-89) of 2013** restructured Pennsylvania transportation funding, with motor fuel tax cap modifications affecting highway revenue.

**Pennsylvania bridge condition.** Pennsylvania has historically had among the highest counts of structurally deficient bridges in the nation. The IIJA Bridge Investment Program was particularly relevant to Pennsylvania's bridge backlog.

### Local architecture

**Philadelphia Streets Department.** Under Mayor Parker's 2024 reorganization, Streets Department retains paving, lighting, traffic engineering, and infrastructure projects responsibilities (with sanitation moved to the new Department of Sanitation per the May 17, 2024 executive order plus memorandum of understanding). **Streets Commissioner: Kristin Del Rossi**, appointed February 2024 (continuing per the December 19, 2025 State of the City). Previously served as the Streets Department's chief engineer and transportation operations director.

The Streets Department's operational scope includes: local-street pavement maintenance and reconstruction; traffic signal operation and maintenance; streetscape improvements (curbs, sidewalks, pedestrian crossings, ADA compliance); bridge maintenance for city-owned bridges (PennDOT maintains state-owned bridges); street lighting.

**Office of Transportation, Infrastructure, and Sustainability (OTIS) / Office of Multimodal Planning (OMP).** Mayor's office unit on transportation and infrastructure planning, parallel to operational departments. **In March 2024, Mayor Parker signed an executive order recommitting Philadelphia to reach zero traffic deaths**; the order directed the Office of Multimodal Planning to assist in developing, reviewing, and publishing all checklists called for in the city's Complete Streets ordinance from 2013 and to update the High Injury Network and set goals for safety improvements to every mile of the HIN by 2030. The Vision Zero Action Plan 2030 (released November 25, 2025) is the operational deliverable on this EO.

**Complete Streets Ordinance (2013).** Philadelphia's Complete Streets policy was originally established by executive order under prior administrations and codified in city ordinance. Mayor Parker's 2024 Vision Zero EO reinforced the ordinance's implementation requirements. Complete Streets operates as a procedural requirement that all street design and reconstruction projects consider multi-modal users (pedestrians, cyclists, transit riders, drivers, freight, accessibility users). Complete Streets project areas have shown 34% fewer fatal/serious-injury crashes and 20% fewer total injury crashes.

**Philadelphia Code provisions.** Title 11 (Streets), Title 12 (Traffic Code), and other Code sections govern street operations, parking, and pedestrian infrastructure.

**Philadelphia Parking Authority (PPA).** State authority overseeing on-street parking and parking enforcement. PPA operates under different governance than city departments, creating coordination questions on streetscape work that affects parking. PPA also has roles in vehicle impoundment under Mayor Parker's Illegal Dumping Task Force and has been authorized to impound vehicles caught in the act of illegal dumping under recent state legislation.

**Vision Zero Action Plan 2030 (released November 25, 2025).** Released by OTIS, Vision Zero Philadelphia, the Department of Public Health (PDPH), the Department of Streets, and the Office of Emergency Management, fulfilling Mayor Parker's March 2024 executive order. The new High Injury Network is based on 2019–2023 crash data and reaffirms that 12% of streets account for 80% of fatal/serious injury crashes. PDPH developed an **[Underserved Communities (UC) metric](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#pdph-uc-metric)** — KSI crash rate 2.4 times higher in highest-UC tracts vs. lowest-UC tracts. Approximately one-third (137 miles) of the HIN is located in highest-UC tracts; only 14% (61 miles) in lowest-UC tracts. Plan commits to safety upgrades on every mile of the HIN by 2030. Pilot of Intelligent Speed Assistance (ISA) in municipal fleet. Five neighborhood slow zones (20 mph limits) completed; concrete pedestrian median islands installed on Race, Chestnut, and Broad Streets.

**Bridge inventory in PA-3.** Schuylkill River crossings, Delaware River crossings (jointly governed with Delaware River Port Authority for the bi-state bridges — Walt Whitman Bridge, Ben Franklin Bridge, etc., though these are not city responsibility per se), and intra-city bridges.

**Federal funding flow at the local level.** Streets Department receives federal-aid funds through DVRPC TIP programming and state-pass-through from PennDOT. CDBG funds (HUD) can be applied to streetscape improvements. The Streets Department's capital budget is supplemented by federal sources for specific projects.

**Home Rule authority and limits.** Philadelphia's Home Rule Charter authorizes the Streets Department's operations on local streets. Federal-aid project requirements (Title VI environmental justice review, NEPA environmental review, ADA Title II compliance) attach to projects with federal funding regardless of local jurisdiction. PennDOT retains state-route jurisdiction within city boundaries — meaning the Streets Department does not have authority to modify state-route design without PennDOT concurrence.

## Geography & representation

**Data provenance.** Tier 1 ACS data for vehicle availability, commute mode, and demographic disaggregation (not retrieved at full Phase 2 detail). Tier 2 Vision Zero Action Plan 2030 (released November 25, 2025); OpenDataPhilly Vision Zero High Injury Network dataset (updated to 2019–2023 crash data basis); Philly Stat 360 Vision Zero dashboard (launched March 24, 2025); PDPH Underserved Communities (UC) metric analysis; DVRPC regional crash and safety data. Tier 3 ZIP-level injury / fatality data drawn from PennDOT crash data and PDPH hospital / vital statistics data linkage. Other sources include PennDOT crash data; FHWA Pennsylvania Division federal-aid project records; Philadelphia Streets Department capital project records; DOJ ADA Title II compliance records (if any); USACE bridge inventory.

**PA-3 statistical profile.** Per the Vision Zero Action Plan 2030 (released November 25, 2025): **2024 traffic fatalities: 120**, down from 123 in 2023. The 5-year average since 2020 is more than 50% higher than the 5-year average prior to 2020. **2024 traffic-related injuries: 362**, down 18% from 442 in 2023. **High Injury Network (HIN): 12% of Philadelphia streets account for 80% of traffic deaths and serious injuries.** Updated HIN based on 2019–2023 crash data (previous HIN: 2014–2018). **Vulnerable road user disparity:** less than 10% of 2024 crashes involved people walking, biking, rolling, or riding a motorcycle, but those modes accounted for nearly two-thirds of fatalities. **Underserved Communities (UC) metric (PDPH):** KSI crash rate is 2.4 times higher in census tracts scoring highest on the UC metric compared to areas with the lowest scores. **HIN concentration:** Nearly one-third (137 miles) of the HIN is located in tracts that score highest on the UC metric. Only 14% (61 miles) of the HIN is in tracts with the lowest UC scores. **Demographic disparities:** PDPH's traffic-related-deaths analysis shows Black and Hispanic Philadelphians experience higher rates of traffic-related deaths.

The Roosevelt Boulevard automated speed enforcement (ASE) program, in place since 2020: 95%+ reduction in speeding violations; 21% reduction in fatal/serious-injury crashes; 50% reduction in pedestrian-involved crashes. ASE has been expanded to PA Route 611 (Broad Street/Old York Road), with approval to extend to PA Route 13, Route 2001 (Erie/Torresdale), Route 2016 (Allegheny), Route 1004 (Richmond/Delaware), and seven school zones. Complete Streets project areas: 34% fewer fatal/serious-injury crashes; 20% fewer total injury crashes.

Federal and state grants for safety projects: Philadelphia has been awarded over $210 million for projects including the Chinatown Stitch, Roosevelt Boulevard intersection modifications, and HIN safety improvements on Old York Road from Erie to Lindley Avenues and Hunting Park Avenue from Wissahickon to Roosevelt Boulevard.

**Geographic variation across four sub-areas.**

- **North/Northwest Philadelphia Core.** The HIN concentration map shows substantial HIN mileage in this sub-area. Roosevelt Boulevard — the historically most dangerous corridor in Philadelphia — runs through North Philadelphia. The Roosevelt Boulevard ASE program's 50% reduction in pedestrian-involved crashes is a sub-area-specific outcome. Other HIN corridors include Broad Street (Route 611), Erie Avenue (Route 2001), Allegheny Avenue (Route 2016), Hunting Park Avenue, Old York Road, and segments of Frankford Avenue (Route 13). The PDPH UC metric concentration and the HIN concentration overlap substantially in this sub-area.
- **West Philadelphia Core.** HIN segments include Lancaster Avenue, Spruce/Pine couplet on the eastern portion, segments of 52nd Street, Walnut Street through University City, Market Street west of 30th. The University City portion has different anchor-institution density than other HIN sub-areas; Penn Police, CHOP, and Drexel security operations create different policing-and-pedestrian-safety dynamics than other West Core neighborhoods. The Cobbs Creek Parkway in the southern portion is a HIN corridor that interacts with the Cobbs Creek watershed (cross-reference Water & Stormwater flood-vulnerability).
- **Northwest Philadelphia.** HIN concentration is lower than in the Cores but not absent. Ridge Avenue, Henry Avenue, and segments of Lincoln Drive carry HIN designation; Wissahickon Avenue is a Vision Zero project corridor. The auto-mode-share dominance in Northwest Philadelphia means crash volume is structurally different — higher vehicle-vehicle crashes, lower pedestrian-vehicle crashes than in the Cores. The lower UC-metric concentration also means lower KSI rates per the 2.4× disparity factor.
- **South/Southwest Philadelphia.** HIN corridors include Broad Street (continuing south through South Philadelphia and into the Sports Complex area), Washington Avenue, Oregon Avenue, segments of Snyder Avenue, and corridors in Southwest Philadelphia along Lindbergh Boulevard, 70th Street, and segments of Woodland Avenue. South Philadelphia's bicycle mode share (rank-2 in two tracts per Census Bureau data) means cyclist-vehicle crash exposure is differentially elevated relative to other sub-areas; the vulnerable-road-user disparity (2/3 of fatalities from <10% of crashes) applies with particular force where cycling mode share is higher.

The geographic variation pattern in this sub-domain is characterized by HIN concentration that closely tracks the PDPH UC metric concentration, which itself tracks the cumulative racial disadvantage geography established in Identity & Legal Status. This tracking is the structural finding: the differential traffic-safety burden across PA-3 sub-areas is not incidental to the underlying demographic geography of the city — it is layered onto it, reproducing the disadvantage pattern in the traffic-safety domain.

**Pathway tracing.** Three aggregate pathways describe how the architecture is encountered by structurally distinct populations.

*Pathway 1 — Pedestrian on HIN corridor.* A pedestrian — typically transit-dependent or short-trip walker — traversing an HIN corridor to access a bus stop, school, healthcare facility, or commercial corridor. Federal-aid highway projects on the corridor are subject to NEPA environmental review and Title VI environmental justice analysis; non-federal-aid local street projects have weaker formal review architecture. The pedestrian's actual safety depends on physical infrastructure (sidewalk presence, crosswalk timing, median refuge islands, pedestrian signals, lighting), traffic control (signal timing, automated speed enforcement), and street design (lane width, vehicle speed compliance). The Vision Zero Action Plan 2030 corridor improvements address some of these elements; the 137-mile HIN mileage in highest-UC-score tracts indicates the scale of the engineering / capital task. Federal Title VI is the formal protection for differential racial impact; ADA Title II is the formal protection for accessibility. PDPH UC metric documentation demonstrates the disparate impact factually. The structural representation question: federal Title VI enforcement could in principle attach to the pattern of HIN concentration in highest-UC-score tracts, treating the differential as a Title VI issue requiring federal review of state-and-local infrastructure investment patterns. This level of Title VI engagement is rare in practice.

*Pathway 2 — Driver on poorly-maintained street.* A vehicle owner-operator navigating local streets that have not received recent repaving or pothole repair, with secondary effects on vehicle wear, repair cost, and crash risk. Streets Department local-street paving prioritization is informed by pavement condition surveys, complaint data (311 calls), and capital budget constraints. Federal-aid highway funding flows primarily to higher-functional-class roadways; local streets are largely city-funded. The driver's experience of poorly-maintained streets reflects city capital constraints (recurring across the physical-infrastructure domain); historical underinvestment patterns concentrate in the North/Northwest Core and West Core sub-areas, with structural reasons traceable to the city's PILOET fiscal architecture (carry-forward from the [Finance &amp; Taxation](/paul/campaign/empower/domain/finance-taxation/) analysis) and to historical disinvestment patterns. Federal IIJA funding for local-street programs is limited; STBG flexibility allows some federal funds to flow to local streets but federal share is typically 80% federal / 20% local match. The structural representation question: does federal House representation engage local-street capital condition as a federal-funding issue, or as a city-budget issue?

*Pathway 3 — Mobility-impaired pedestrian.* A pedestrian using a wheelchair, walker, or other mobility device, navigating sidewalks, curb ramps, pedestrian signals, and bus stops. ADA Title II requires accessibility of public infrastructure including sidewalks, curb ramps, pedestrian signals, and transit stops. The U.S. Access Board's PROWAG provides design standards. Federal-aid highway projects are subject to ADA Title II compliance review; non-federal-aid local street projects also have ADA Title II obligations but with weaker enforcement architecture. DOJ Title II enforcement against municipalities typically arises through complaint-driven investigations or class-action litigation. The mobility-impaired pedestrian's actual experience depends on the cumulative state of sidewalks, curb ramps, and pedestrian-signal compliance across their route — including in Philadelphia's pre-1940 housing stock neighborhoods where sidewalk vintage is older and curb ramp installation has been incremental rather than systematic. Federal House representation's lever on this is DOJ Civil Rights Division engagement, FHWA / FTA federal-aid project ADA compliance attention, and IIJA reauthorization safety provisions.

**Representation question.** Federal law provides extensive infrastructure for road, bridge, and pedestrian safety: Title 23 federal-aid highway program (NHPP, STBG, HSIP, BIP, CRP); FHWA project-level oversight; ADA Title II accessibility requirements; Title VI environmental justice review for federal-aid projects; NEPA environmental review for federal actions; Section 4(f) protections for parks and historic sites. Pennsylvania law provides PennDOT state-route authority, the Motor License Fund constitutional dedication for highway purposes, and Act 89 transportation funding framework. Philadelphia Home Rule provides Streets Department local-street authority; the Complete Streets Ordinance (codified after the 2013 executive order) requires multi-modal consideration in street design; Mayor Parker's 2024 Vision Zero Executive Order strengthened Action Plan / Capital Plan development and HIN-update processes.

What PA-3 residents experience is road and pedestrian infrastructure through fatality and injury rates that reflect the HIN concentration pattern. 80% of traffic deaths and serious injuries occur on 12% of streets. The HIN concentrates in highest-UC-score tracts (137 of approximately 198 HIN miles). KSI rates are 2.4× higher in highest-UC-score areas than in lowest-UC-score areas. Black and Hispanic Philadelphians experience higher traffic-related-death rates. Vulnerable road users (pedestrians, cyclists, motorcyclists) account for two-thirds of fatalities while representing under 10% of crashes. Sidewalk condition, curb ramp completeness, and pedestrian signal accessibility vary by sub-area in patterns that track the HIN-and-UC concentration.

The mismatch between formal provision and actual experience is structural. Federal-aid project requirements (Title VI, ADA Title II, NEPA) attach to specific projects with federal funding; the cumulative pattern of HIN concentration emerges from many decisions over decades, no single one of which would necessarily have triggered Title VI flagging at the project level. The historical investment pattern that produced today's HIN concentration in highest-UC-score tracts is a product of state-route prioritization, local capital constraints, and demographic geography compounding over time. The 2.4× KSI disparity is the cumulative output of this layered process. Federal-aid highway funding flows through PennDOT for state-route projects within Philadelphia and through DVRPC TIP programming for federal-aid metropolitan projects; local-street capital is largely city-funded with limited federal share. The structural vulnerability: federal floor protections (Title VI, ADA Title II) operate at project level but produce uneven coverage across the cumulative network, and PDPH's UC-metric documentation of the cumulative disparity is a relatively recent analytical capacity that did not exist for most of the period during which the HIN pattern was forming.

Federal House representation for PA-3 has direct levers on the federal-aid program portion of the system: IIJA reauthorization (with the September 30, 2026 expiration as an inflection point); FHWA project-level oversight; HSIP funding levels; BIP bridge funding allocation; ADA Title II enforcement through DOJ Civil Rights Division engagement; Title VI cumulative-impact analysis advocacy. Federal representation has indirect levers on the state-architecture portion through congressional delegation coordination with state legislators (e.g., on Motor License Fund integrity) and through federal-funding conditions that affect state behavior. Federal representation has no direct authority over Philadelphia Streets Department operations but can use federal funding relationships to support specific HIN projects, ASE expansion, and Complete Streets implementation. The structural representation question is whether federal House representation engages the cumulative HIN-UC-metric pattern as a federal-floor issue (which would require sustained Title VI cumulative-impact attention beyond standard project-level review), or treats traffic safety as a city-administered domain in which federal involvement is funding-based but not enforcement-based.

<h2 id="gaps">Gap analysis</h2>

**Gap 1 — Title VI cumulative-impact analysis for HIN-UC concentration.** PDPH's Underserved Communities (UC) metric documents that approximately one-third (137 miles) of HIN mileage is concentrated in highest-UC-score tracts; KSI crash rate is 2.4× higher in highest-UC areas; Black and Hispanic Philadelphians experience higher rates of traffic-related deaths. Federal Title VI environmental justice review at the project level produces uneven coverage across the cumulative network; cumulative-impact analysis at the network level is structurally distinct from project-level review and is not currently a standard federal practice for surface transportation infrastructure. Federal House representation can advocate for cumulative-impact Title VI analysis at FHWA and DOT, and can engage DOJ Civil Rights Division on the structural pattern.

**Gap 2 — ADA Title II sidewalk and curb ramp compliance pace.** ADA Title II requires accessibility of public infrastructure including sidewalks, curb ramps, pedestrian signals, and transit stops. Philadelphia's pre-1940 housing stock concentration corresponds to older sidewalk infrastructure with incremental ADA retrofitting rather than systematic compliance. DOJ Title II enforcement against municipalities typically arises through complaint-driven investigations or class-action litigation rather than proactive systematic enforcement. DOJ Civil Rights Division engagement and federal-aid project ADA compliance attention are levers.

**Gap 3 — Local-street capital federal funding structural limit.** Federal-aid highway funds flow primarily to higher-functional-class roadways (Interstate, principal arterial); local streets are largely city-funded with limited federal share. STBG flexibility allows some federal funds to flow to local streets but federal share is typically 80%/20% with non-federal match required. Philadelphia Streets Department local-street capital condition reflects city capital constraints and historical disinvestment patterns concentrated in the Cores. Federal-aid program design advocacy for local-street eligibility expansion, plus STBG flexibility maintenance in reauthorization, are levers.

**Gap 4 — Vision Zero state-legislation needs.** The Vision Zero Action Plan 2030 identifies actions requiring state legislation for full implementation: granting the city more authority over speed limits; deploying additional traffic cameras beyond current authorizations; adjusting the curb definition in the state Vehicle Code to allow parking-protected pedestrian lanes; authorizing additional bike lane configurations. These are state-policy gaps rather than federal-policy gaps but interact with federal HSIP and Vision Zero funding eligibility. Federal House representation has indirect levers through congressional delegation coordination with state legislators; federal program eligibility can shape state implementation choices.

**Gap 5 — Bridge inventory PA-3-specific data.** Pennsylvania has historically had among the highest counts of structurally deficient bridges nationally; the IIJA Bridge Investment Program addressed the PA bridge backlog. PA-3-specific bridge inventory by condition rating, including the city-owned vs. PennDOT-owned split within Philadelphia and the Schuylkill/Delaware River bridge population, is not surfaced at sufficient detail in the current analysis. Federal Bridge Investment Program advocacy for specific PA-3 bridge projects, plus reauthorization-period BIP funding-level engagement, are levers.

**Gap 6 — HIN federal funding pipeline post-IIJA.** The Vision Zero Action Plan 2030 has identified $210M+ in federal and state grants for safety projects covering specific HIN corridors (Chinatown Stitch, Roosevelt Boulevard intersections, Old York Road and Hunting Park Avenue improvements). Continuation of HIN safety funding past the September 30, 2026 IIJA expiration depends on reauthorization outcome. Reauthorization advocacy for HSIP and Safe Streets and Roads for All funding levels, plus specific PA-3 HIN project advocacy, are levers.
