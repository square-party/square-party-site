# Solid Waste & Sanitation

<p class="domain-lead">Philadelphia spends about $1.5 million a year cleaning up illegally dumped material — between 6,800 and 11,500 tons annually. Mayor Parker's 2024 reorganization split Sanitation from the Streets Department into a new Department of Sanitation reporting through the Office of Clean and Green Initiatives, expanded the Taking Care of Business Clean Corridors Program from 49 to 129 corridors, funded 1,500 additional Bigbelly trash cans, and launched the One Philly, A United City Illegal Dumping Task Force in November 2025. Enforcement scale runs structurally short of dumping volume: the Philadelphia Police Department's Environmental Crimes Unit operates with two staff; recent annual arrests for dumping have not exceeded ~31, and roughly half of adjudicated cases are withdrawn or dismissed — which is what drove the shift toward SWEEP officer Notice of Violation enforcement on a civil-standard rather than criminal-standard basis. The geographic distribution of illegal dumping tracks the cumulative racial disadvantage geography of PA-3, with Billy Penn's 2022 investigation documenting concentration in poorer neighborhoods with higher proportions of Black and Latino residents — a pattern produced by the intersection of vacant-lot concentration (cross-reference the housing analysis), commercial dumping by out-of-neighborhood actors using low-enforcement areas, and historical disinvestment. Federal RCRA Subtitle D establishes minimum standards but does not require active prevention of illegal dumping or fund municipal enforcement; federal levers reach SD4 primarily through <a href="/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#cdbg">CDBG</a>, EPA Brownfields, and CERCLA Superfund — the most consequential federal-floor question being whether EPA Brownfields program scale can meaningfully address the vacant-lot-driven dumping concentration.</p>

## Legal Architecture

### Constitutional and statutory grounding

Federal authority over solid waste rests on the Commerce Clause (interstate movement of waste; interstate hazardous substances) and the Spending Clause (federal financial assistance for state solid waste programs). The Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) and CERCLA both rest on these grounds.

Pennsylvania constitutional authority for solid waste rests on [Article I § 27](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#article-i-27) (Environmental Rights Amendment) — public natural resources held in trust extends to ambient environmental quality affected by solid waste management — and on Article IX (Local Government / Home Rule) for local solid waste authority. Article I § 27 is particularly relevant to siting decisions for waste disposal facilities and to environmental-justice analysis of waste-handling impacts on lower-income and minority neighborhoods.

The constitutional tension specific to solid waste: federal RCRA establishes minimum standards (Subtitle D for non-hazardous; Subtitle C for hazardous) but defers most non-hazardous waste authority to states. State authority is then frequently delegated to local entities. This creates a layered structure in which environmental-justice failures can occur within the legal framework rather than as violations of it — siting decisions and operational decisions can be RCRA-compliant while producing concentrated environmental impacts on specific neighborhoods. Article I § 27 provides a state-constitutional review layer that, post-*Robinson Township*, may apply substantive constraints not present in federal RCRA.

### Federal architecture

**[Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA)](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#rcra)** at 42 U.S.C. § 6901 et seq. is the primary federal solid waste statute. **Subtitle D** governs non-hazardous solid waste — largely state-implemented; EPA establishes minimum criteria (40 CFR Parts 257 and 258 for landfills) and approves state programs. **Subtitle C** governs hazardous waste — more directly federally enforced, with EPA retaining authority where state programs are not adequate.

EPA Region III oversees RCRA implementation in Pennsylvania, with PA DEP holding most operational authority under delegated programs.

**[CERCLA](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#cercla) / Superfund** at 42 U.S.C. § 9601 et seq. addresses cleanup of hazardous substance contamination at sites of past contamination. CERCLA-relevant cross-reference here includes the Lower Darby Creek Area Superfund Site adjacent to Eastwick / Southwest Philadelphia.

### State architecture

**Pennsylvania Solid Waste Management Act**, 35 P.S. § 6018.101 et seq. — primary Pennsylvania statute implementing RCRA Subtitle D, going beyond federal requirements in some areas. Addresses municipal waste, residual waste, and hazardous waste, with provisions for permitting, operations, financial assurance, and enforcement.

**Municipal Waste Planning, Recycling and Waste Reduction Act (Act 101 of 1988)**, 53 P.S. § 4000.101 et seq. — establishes Pennsylvania's recycling framework, requiring counties and certain municipalities (including Philadelphia) to implement source-separation recycling programs.

**PA DEP Southeast Regional Office** in Norristown is the state regulatory agency for solid waste in Philadelphia. PA DEP issues solid waste facility permits, conducts inspections, and enforces state and federal solid waste regulations.

**Pennsylvania-specific features.** Pennsylvania has historically been a major importer of out-of-state municipal solid waste (received from New York, New Jersey, and other states) due to landfill capacity. This creates regulatory tensions between state interest in protecting Pennsylvania residents from environmental impacts and Commerce Clause-based federal protections of interstate solid waste flows.

### Local architecture

**Philadelphia Department of Sanitation.** Established as a functionally distinct unit through Mayor Cherelle Parker's 2024 executive action splitting Sanitation from the Streets Department. The Philadelphia Home Rule Charter formally specifies that the Sanitation Division is part of the Streets Department; the 2024 reorganization operates through executive order plus memorandum of understanding signed May 17, 2024, using a Home Rule Charter provision allowing employees or divisions to be loaned to other offices. Permanent reorganization would require charter amendment via Council vote and voter approval.

The Sanitation Department reports through the **Office of Clean and Green Initiatives**, headed by **Carlton Williams** (former Streets Commissioner under Mayor Kenney). **Sanitation Commissioner: Crystal Jacobs Shipman.** Appointed February 2024; continuing per the December 19, 2025 State of the City. Previously served as a department spokesperson and the executive director of public safety programs in the Streets Department's transportation division.

**Sanitation operational scope:** residential trash collection (weekly citywide; twice-weekly in Center City and parts of South Philadelphia rolled out December 2024); recycling collection (single-stream); street cleaning (citywide cleanup programs rolled out 2024); illegal dumping response.

**Office of Clean and Green Initiatives.** Created by Mayor Parker; coordinates quality-of-life and cleanup efforts among city agencies, community partners, and businesses. The 38-member Clean and Green Cabinet was formally established by executive order in May 2024. The City's FY25 budget (effective July 2024) increased funding to the Taking Care of Business Clean Corridors Program, channeling money through City Council member offices to private organizations tasked with cleaning 129 corridors (up from 49). The budget also funded an additional 1,500 Bigbelly trash cans on sidewalks. TCB reports a 50 percent reduction in litter along corridors cleaned by TCB contractors since 2022 (per the 2025 State of the City).

**Illegal Dumping Task Force.** Mayor Parker and the Office of Clean and Green Initiatives announced the formation of the **One Philly, A United City Illegal Dumping Task Force** in October 2025, launched November 3, 2025. Members include intergovernmental partners: Office of Clean and Green, Licenses and Inspections, Philadelphia Law Department, Philadelphia Police Department, Philadelphia Parking Authority, Philadelphia Parks &amp; Recreation, Department of Sanitation, Department of Streets, Community Life Improvement Program (CLIP), and the District Attorney's Office. The Streets Department installs surveillance cameras that aid in capturing and prosecuting offenders. Recent state legislation authorizes the Philadelphia Parking Authority (PPA) to impound vehicles caught in the act of illegal dumping.

**[SWEEP](/paul/campaign/empower/glossary/#sweep) Officers (Department of Sanitation) and Park Rangers (Parks &amp; Recreation)** received NOV (Notice of Violation) training from Licenses and Inspections in partnership with the Law Department, Philadelphia Police Department, and CLIP. The NOV process places the burden of proof on dumpers (rather than the higher burden of criminal prosecution) and allows for stiffer penalties.

**Federal funding flow at the local level.** RCRA does not provide significant direct federal financial assistance to municipalities for solid waste operations. CDBG can fund some sanitation-related infrastructure (e.g., trash receptacles in commercial corridors). Brownfields program (EPA + HUD interface) provides assessment and cleanup grants for contaminated sites. Most sanitation operations are locally-funded through the General Fund and through commercial/residential service fees.

**Home Rule authority and limits.** Philadelphia's Home Rule Charter authorizes sanitation operations as an exercise of police power. State PA DEP regulatory authority constrains how solid waste is handled (transfer station siting, disposal facility selection). Federal RCRA Subtitle D requirements set minimum standards. The 2024 Sanitation/Streets reorganization operates within Home Rule Charter constraints (using the "loaning" provision rather than charter amendment), which means the reorganization could be reversed by future executive action without charter amendment.

## Geography & representation

**Data provenance.** Tier 1 ACS housing tenure data and household characteristics relevant here because illegal dumping intersects with vacant lot concentration (cross-reference housing) and trash collection patterns intersect with housing density. Tier 2 OpenDataPhilly 311 service request dataset; illegal dumping tonnage tracked by Streets Department and Department of Sanitation; neighborhood Litter Index (per Clean Air Council reference); recycling participation by collection route. Tier 3 ZIP-level 311 dumping report counts, Bigbelly placement, Clean Corridor program participation. Other sources include Billy Penn investigation of Philadelphia illegal dumping (March 2022 / April 2023); Office of Clean and Green Initiatives documentation; Mayor Parker administration reorganization documents; Philadelphia Police Department Environmental Crimes Unit documentation; District Attorney's Office prosecution data.

**Illegal dumping scale.** The City of Philadelphia spends approximately $1.5 million annually cleaning up illegally dumped material. Illegal dumping pickups by city crews fluctuated significantly: from a recent high of over 11,500 tons in 2016, pickups declined annually to approximately 6,800 tons in 2019, then increased again in 2021 according to Billy Penn's analysis. Illegal dumping complaints are reportedly among the highest 311 calls from residents.

**Enforcement architecture and capacity.** Philadelphia Police Department's Environmental Crimes Unit, in the Major Crimes Division, is a two-person unit (one detective, one officer) responsible for environmental crime investigation including illegal dumping. About half of PPD dumping investigations stem from 311 complaints; the rest start with patrol officers or sanitation workers observing newly dumped trash in person or via surveillance cameras. The City has 250 fixed-location and mobile cameras around Philadelphia for dumping and quality-of-life enforcement, with potential access to footage from thousands of private cameras. Despite this enforcement infrastructure, in recent years police have not arrested more than 31 people annually for illegal dumping (2019 figure). Of the 20 dumping cases adjudicated in 2019, half were withdrawn or dismissed per the District Attorney's Office.

**Mayor Parker administration response.** The 2024 reorganization split Sanitation from the Streets Department, creating the Department of Sanitation (Commissioner Crystal Jacobs Shipman) reporting through the Office of Clean and Green Initiatives (headed by Carlton Williams). The 38-member Clean and Green Cabinet was formally established by executive order in May 2024. The FY25 budget increased TCB Clean Corridors Program from 49 to 129 corridors and funded 1,500 additional Bigbelly trash cans. The Illegal Dumping Task Force (One Philly, A United City) was announced October 2025 and launched November 3, 2025. SWEEP officers and Park Rangers received NOV training, shifting the enforcement burden of proof to civil rather than criminal standard.

**Pilot programming.** The Office of Clean and Green Initiatives, with William Penn Foundation funding, launched a pilot program with SEAMAAC and the Overbrook Environmental Education Center to investigate root causes of illegal dumping in specific neighborhoods.

**Geographic variation across four sub-areas.**

- **North/Northwest Philadelphia Core.** Per Billy Penn analysis, dumping concentrations are well-documented in the North/Northwest Core, including specific intersections in Strawberry Mansion, Allegheny West, and adjacent neighborhoods. Vacant lot concentration in this sub-area provides the structural opportunity geography for recurrent dumping at specific lots. Concentrated commercial corridors with TCB Clean Corridors coverage (Lehigh Avenue, Broad Street segments, Erie Avenue) receive targeted cleaning attention; off-corridor blocks receive less.
- **West Philadelphia Core.** Cobbs Creek, Mantua, and Mill Creek areas show dumping concentration. Anchor institutional adjacency (Penn, Drexel, CHOP) does not extend across the entirety of the West Core; cleanup resources are differential between anchor-adjacent and non-anchor-adjacent blocks.
- **Northwest Philadelphia.** Lower dumping concentration than in the Cores, but specific commercial-corridor edges (e.g., parts of Wissahickon Avenue, Henry Avenue) and post-industrial sites within the sub-area do receive recurring dumping. Manayunk's Schuylkill River edge and the Wissahickon Watershed boundary areas (boundary-adjacent observation: Wissahickon Valley Park extends into PA-2) include specific dumping hot spots.
- **South/Southwest Philadelphia.** Per Billy Penn: "8th and McKean in South Philadelphia" identified as a hot spot; Southwest Philadelphia broadly identified as a concentration zone. The William Penn Foundation pilot's SEAMAAC partnership operates in South Philadelphia. Eastwick / Southwest Philadelphia has Lower Darby Creek Superfund Site adjacency, which creates a complex environmental geography in which historical waste disposal intersects with current illegal dumping concerns. Twice-weekly trash pickup rollout (December 2024) covered "Center City and parts of South Philadelphia," providing differential service intensity in some South Philadelphia areas.

The geographic variation pattern in this sub-domain tracks closely with the cumulative racial disadvantage geography established in the identity / legal status analysis — Billy Penn's observation that "dumping and litter tend to be worse in poorer neighborhoods with higher proportions of Black and Latino residents" is a direct empirical statement of this tracking. The geographic distribution of illegal dumping is therefore not separable from the historical geography of disinvestment, vacant lot concentration, and racial residential segregation. Across the water, roads, and waste sub-domains, the same underlying demographic geography produces differential infrastructure burden.

**Pathway tracing.**

*Pathway 1 — Resident in illegal dumping hot spot.* A resident on a block with concentrated vacant lots or proximity to identified dumping hot spots, encountering illegally dumped trash on their street or sidewalk on a recurring basis. The institutional encounter sequence: 311 reporting (Philly311 system); SWEEP officer NOV process for documented violations; Streets Department / Sanitation Department cleanup response; Police Environmental Crimes Unit investigation if dumping is observed. The resident's actual experience depends on cleanup response time (which varies by service area workload), repeat-dumping pattern (vacant lots receive recurrent dumping; one-time cleanups do not solve the structural problem), and enforcement effectiveness (the 2-person Environmental Crimes Unit and ~31 max annual arrests indicate enforcement scale is small relative to dumping volume). The structural mismatch: dumping is a high-volume problem (~6,800–11,500 tons/year), enforcement is low-arrest (~31/year max), and prosecution attrition is significant (half of 2019 cases withdrawn/dismissed).

*Pathway 2 — Resident with sanitation service variation.* A resident living in an area with weekly trash pickup (most of the city) versus a resident in Center City or parts of South Philadelphia with twice-weekly pickup (rolled out December 2024). Weekly pickup means a 7-day accumulation cycle; twice-weekly pickup means a 3–4 day cycle. Concentrated commercial activity, dense rowhome blocks, and high-foot-traffic neighborhoods benefit differentially from twice-weekly pickup. The structural feature: service variation is administered by neighborhood-level decisions about pickup frequency, with criteria including foot traffic, commercial activity, and neighborhood characteristics. The structural representation question: who decides the geography of twice-weekly pickup expansion, and on what criteria?

*Pathway 3 — Recycler / waste-reduction-engaged resident.* A resident engaged in single-stream recycling, composting, and waste reduction practices. The institutional encounter sequence: single-stream recycling collection per scheduled pickup; recycling processing through the regional waste processing infrastructure; downstream sale of recyclables on commodity markets; community composting initiatives (limited city-level infrastructure; primarily community-led). Federal RCRA Subtitle D establishes the floor; PA Act 101 of 1988 requires county and certain municipal recycling programs. Philadelphia is mandated by Act 101. Federal House representation has limited direct levers on recycling operations but can engage RCRA reauthorization, EPA recycling infrastructure programs, and federal recycling-market support programs.

**Representation question.** Federal RCRA Subtitle D establishes minimum standards for non-hazardous solid waste management, with state implementation. EPA Region III oversight in Philadelphia. CERCLA addresses cleanup of past contamination at sites including the Lower Darby Creek Area Superfund Site. Pennsylvania law adds the Solid Waste Management Act, Act 101 of 1988 recycling requirements, and Article I § 27 substantive constitutional review. PA DEP Southeast Regional Office provides state regulatory authority. Philadelphia Home Rule authorizes the Department of Sanitation (operating through 2024 executive reorganization), the Office of Clean and Green Initiatives, the SWEEP officer NOV process, and the Police Environmental Crimes Unit's enforcement role.

What PA-3 residents experience is solid waste, sanitation, and illegal dumping through dumping hot spots concentrated in poorer neighborhoods with higher proportions of Black and Latino residents; 311 reporting that yields cleanup response but with recurring re-dumping at vacant-lot hot spots; enforcement that is institutionally underscaled (2-person Environmental Crimes Unit; ~31 arrests/year max); twice-weekly pickup expansion concentrated in higher-density commercial-active areas; recycling participation that varies with housing type and neighborhood characteristics. The Office of Clean and Green Initiatives expansion under Mayor Parker represents recent operational expansion; cumulative effect on the dumping concentration pattern is not yet documented.

The mismatch between formal provision and actual experience is structural. Federal RCRA Subtitle D provides minimum standards but does not require active prevention of illegal dumping or enforcement architecture. State PA SWMA and Act 101 provide additional state-level requirements but do not address dumping enforcement scale. Local enforcement capacity is structurally underscaled relative to dumping volume; the structural account for this scale mismatch involves long-term municipal budget constraints (cross-reference to the [Finance &amp; Taxation](/paul/campaign/empower/domain/finance-taxation/) analysis), the categorization of illegal dumping as a quality-of-life rather than serious-crime issue, and the federal floor's silence on enforcement. The geographic concentration of dumping in poorer Black and Latino neighborhoods reflects: vacant lot concentration in those neighborhoods; commercial dumping by out-of-neighborhood actors using those areas as low-enforcement-cost dumping grounds; and historical disinvestment producing infrastructure conditions.

Federal House representation for PA-3 has direct levers on the federal portions of this sub-domain: RCRA reauthorization advocacy, EPA Brownfields program funding (relevant to vacant lot remediation that intersects with dumping geography), CERCLA Superfund work (relevant to Lower Darby Creek and any other PA-3 Superfund issues), and EPA recycling infrastructure programs. Federal representation has indirect levers on state-level structures through congressional delegation coordination. Federal representation has no direct authority over Philadelphia DOS / OCGI operations but can engage federal-funding-conditioned compliance monitoring. The most consequential federal-floor question may be the relationship between EPA Brownfields program scale and vacant-lot-driven dumping concentration — a relationship that, if engaged, would link federal environmental program scale to local quality-of-life outcomes in a structurally tractable way.

<h2 id="gaps">Gap analysis</h2>

**Gap 1 — Enforcement-scale gap relative to dumping volume.** PPD Environmental Crimes Unit operates with two staff for citywide environmental crime investigation including illegal dumping. Maximum recent annual arrests for dumping have not exceeded ~31; of 20 cases adjudicated in 2019, half were withdrawn or dismissed. Annual dumping pickup volume ranged from ~6,800 tons (2019) to over 11,500 tons (2016 peak). The structural mismatch between enforcement capacity and dumping volume is significant. The shift toward SWEEP officer NOV (Notice of Violation) civil-standard enforcement under Mayor Parker's Illegal Dumping Task Force represents an alternative enforcement architecture but does not change the criminal-enforcement capacity. Federal program development could fund municipal anti-dumping enforcement, but no current federal program directly does so.

**Gap 2 — Federal anti-dumping program absence.** RCRA Subtitle D establishes minimum standards for non-hazardous solid waste management but does not require active prevention of illegal dumping or fund municipal dumping enforcement. There is no federal program directly funding municipal illegal-dumping enforcement at scale. Federal funding for solid waste reaches Philadelphia primarily through CDBG (limited dumping-related uses), Brownfields (vacant-lot remediation that intersects with dumping geography), and CERCLA Superfund (historical contamination, not active dumping). Federal House representation can advocate for federal anti-dumping program development; this is agenda-setting work without a current legislative pathway.

**Gap 3 — Vacant lot remediation scale relative to dumping geography.** Vacant lot concentration produces the structural opportunity geography for illegal dumping. The historical pattern of demolition-without-redevelopment in the North/Northwest Core has produced concentrated vacant lot frontage. EPA Brownfields program (assessment and cleanup grants) addresses contaminated vacant sites but the program scale is limited relative to Philadelphia's overall vacant lot count. Federal Brownfields program funding advocacy and cross-domain coordination with housing/property work are levers.

**Gap 4 — Twice-weekly pickup expansion criteria transparency.** Twice-weekly residential trash pickup was rolled out December 2024 in "Center City and parts of South Philadelphia." Expansion criteria — what qualifies a neighborhood for twice-weekly pickup vs. weekly — were not surfaced at the level of formal published criteria. Differential service intensity affects illegal-dumping-opportunity geography. Not directly federal, but federal funding could potentially support expansion of twice-weekly pickup in high-need areas if structured through CDBG or analogous program.

**Gap 5 — Recycling participation data by neighborhood.** Single-stream recycling collection operates citywide but participation varies by neighborhood, housing type, and other factors. Neighborhood-level recycling participation data was not surfaced in the current analysis. The relationship between recycling participation, contamination rates, and Act 101 compliance reporting is structurally relevant to program design. Federal EPA recycling infrastructure programs (developed under IIJA) provide one pathway; federal recycling-market-support programs are a longer-term advocacy position.
