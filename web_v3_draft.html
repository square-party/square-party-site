The American Experiment

Did we stop running it?

An experiment requires three things: a hypothesis, a method, and someone paying attention to the results. The United States was founded as all three. The hypothesis was that ordinary people could govern themselves. The method was constitutional democracy. And the experimenters were the citizens themselves.

Trust in the federal government has declined from 77% in 1964 to 17% as of September 2025 (Pew Research Center). This is a sixty-year secular trend that has persisted through every administration, every economic cycle, every war and peace. It is structural.

Among young adults aged 18–24, the most-cited barrier to participation is not apathy but feeling uninformed. 80% of those who score high on civic knowledge plan to engage civically, versus only 40% of those who score low. Knowledge isn't just correlated with participation — it's a prerequisite.

The founders didn't promise us a good government. They gave us the tools to build one and said your turn. The experiment isn't over. It's just not being run.

Four Forces Behind the Drift

The complexity barrier. Democracy accumulated complexity as it tried to solve complex problems — raising the barrier to participation until most people couldn't meaningfully participate. The experiment generated conditions that excluded the experimenters.

The incentive structure. Safe seats reward ideological intensity over governance competence. Media rewards conflict over deliberation. Campaign finance shifts the relevant constituency from voters to donors.

The identity trap. Political identity shifted from "what I want the government to do" to "who I am." When politics is identity, compromise is betrayal and the other side isn't wrong — they're bad.

Manufactured disengagement. Voter suppression, gerrymandering, underfunded civic education, and disinformation campaigns are deliberate strategies to reduce participation. Not all of the drift is emergent. Some of it is being steered.

The Opening

The data shows that people are disconnected, not opposed. 68% of young adults believe their vote matters. 51% describe themselves as near the ideological middle. 61% don't identify with either major party. This is a population that has given up on the current implementation of self-governance — not the idea itself. The raw material for re-engagement exists. The question is whether the system can be redesigned to make engagement meaningful.

Two projects follow from this diagnosis. Each addresses a different dimension of the structural problem.

The Map Is the Territory

The representation problem — and what to do about it

George Washington used his only substantive intervention at the Constitutional Convention to argue for more representation. Today each House member serves 761,000 constituents. The House was frozen at 435 in 1929 — not as democratic theory, but because rural states blocked reapportionment.

Political scientist Rein Taagepera observed that effective legislatures worldwide scale as the cube root of their population. For the U.S., that predicts ~690 members. The gap between what we have and what representation requires is not marginal. It is structural.

Three Sequential Proposals

I. Constituent Organizing — Start Now

Any candidate subdivides their district into 20–30 issue-based constituent groups, each with a volunteer coordinator. When policy questions arise, constituents respond to short surveys. The representative publishes accountability reports showing what constituents said, how they voted, and why. No new laws required.

II. The Convention Question

People with power don't dilute their own power. Article V's convention mechanism — never used but close several times — offers a pathway for structural reform that Congress has no incentive to pursue itself. Three approaches: a limited convention with pre-ratification safeguards, a shadow convention as proof of concept, or an amendment-first strategy that establishes procedural rules before invoking the mechanism.

III. A Ground-Up Redesign

Tripartite at three levels. Three executives (Administrator, Diplomat, Arbiter — modeled on the Swiss Federal Council and Roman consuls). Three legislatures (Senate, House, and a new People's Assembly at 1:100,000). Three judiciaries (Case Law, Arbitration, External Affairs — modeled on the French and German systems). Every precedent has been tested. Some for centuries.

Capitalism Is a Game That Can Be Won

And a won game is a dead game

Capitalism, as currently structured, permits a win state — a point at which a player has accumulated so much capital, market control, and political influence that they've exited the competitive dynamics that make the system function. They're not playing the game anymore. They've won it.

Every successful competitive system has solved this problem. The NFL shares revenue and caps salaries — not to punish great teams, but because a league where one team wins every year is a league no one watches. The result: the most profitable sports league on Earth.

Rules are not anti-game. Rules are the game. Without them, you don't have competition — you have a parking lot fight.

Game Maintenance: Five Principles

⚖

Dynamic Balancing

Rules that respond to conditions like a thermostat. Tie regulatory intensity to concentration metrics.

🔄

Competition Preservation

Antitrust for the networked age. Prevent dominance in one domain from converting to dominance everywhere.

🏗

Game Infrastructure

Education, healthcare, broadband — not welfare, but the court the game is played on.

🔍

Transparency

In poker, you can bluff. You can't mark the cards. Full lobbying disclosure, algorithmic accountability.

📊

Scorecard Expansion

Supplement GDP with game-health metrics. A scoreboard that only tracks the winner isn't a scoreboard.

🤝

Non-Partisan

"Is the game still playable?" doesn't ask whether you lean left or right. It asks if you want the system to work.

The Next Game

Every competitive system that has lasted eventually faced a question its designers didn't anticipate: what do you do when someone actually wins? Chess has checkmate. Football seasons end. Monopoly — the board game — ends when one player holds everything and the others go home.

American capitalism was not designed to end. It was built before anyone could actually win — before a single entity could hold more wealth than most nations, before capital could compound automatically, generating returns independent of what its owner builds, produces, or contributes to the market.

We've entered territory the designers never mapped. We have, for the first time in history, clear winners — not people doing exceptionally well at the game, but people who have functionally exited it. Whose wealth is self-generating. Who no longer need to compete, innovate, or respond to market pressure the way everyone else does.

This isn't a moral failure of the winners. It's a design gap. We built a marathon with no finish line — and no plan for when someone laps the field and keeps running.

What History Did With Its Winners

Three Historical Models

Noblesse oblige. European aristocracies operated on the premise that great wealth carried great obligation — to maintain the village, fund the commons, keep infrastructure functional. It failed because the obligation was voluntary, and voluntarism fails at scale. But the underlying logic wasn't wrong: wealth is downstream of a functional society. The winner has a direct stake in maintaining what they won within.

The Carnegie principle. "The man who dies rich dies disgraced." Accumulation was acceptable. Perpetual hoarding was not. Carnegie built 2,509 libraries. He was also a ruthless monopolist who violently suppressed his workers. The model is morally incomplete — but the core premise, that winning creates obligation to the game itself, is worth serious examination.

The Norwegian model. When Norway struck oil, the government asked: who does this belong to? The answer was everyone, including people not yet born. The result is the Government Pension Fund Global — one of the largest sovereign wealth funds in the world — held in trust for future generations. Norway designed the next game before the first one ended.

A Framework for the Next Game

The next game doesn't require philanthropy or redistribution in the pejorative sense. It can be structured as a logical continuation — a higher level of play, where the challenge shifts from accumulation to stewardship. Not punishment. The next level.

🏆

Win Recognition

When capital becomes self-sustaining independent of competition, the rules shift. Like an athlete who earns a spot on the coaching staff — same game, different role.

🌱

Stewardship Mode

The winner's new objective: maintain the conditions that made winning possible. Competitive markets. A playable field. A functional commons the next entrant can actually enter.

🔁

Regenerative Design

Sovereign wealth structures, public endowments, investment in game infrastructure. These tools already exist in capitalism's own history. They're the rules of the next level.

A game that can only be won once isn't a game — it's a heist. The most pro-capitalist position available is to design the next level before the current one collapses under the weight of its own winners.

Nothing here requires the abolition of private property or state ownership. Every mechanism already exists in capitalism's own history. The most pro-capitalist thing you can do is keep the game alive.

The Math Is the Math

Fossil fuels are finite. Plan accordingly.

This is not an argument about climate change. It is an argument about arithmetic. Fossil fuels exist in measurable, finite quantities. When they're burned, they're gone. They do not regenerate on any timescale relevant to human civilization.

These numbers have caveats — in both directions. New reserves may be discovered. Consumption is rising, not stable. But no caveat changes the core fact: the number is not infinity. It is a specific, finite, measurable quantity.

If a resource is finite, and you depend on it, and no substitute is deployed at scale, then at some point you face a crisis. The only variable is whether it's planned or unplanned.

The entire argument rests on three facts and one inference. Fact 1: Fossil fuels are finite. Fact 2: We depend on them. Fact 3: No adequate replacement is currently deployed at scale. Inference: We need a plan.

If you disagree, identify which fact is wrong. Not exaggerated. Wrong. The essay doesn't prescribe a specific plan — it prescribes the commitment to planning, organized around five principles: acknowledge the timeline, invest while the current system works, protect affected communities, use current revenue to fund the transition, and measure and adapt.

The answer to "when do we start planning?" has never, in the history of resource management, been "later."

An Honest Discussion

What the law says. What the system does.
The Law — Definitions & Sources

U.S. Law — Definition of Refugee
"...any person who is outside any country of such person's nationality...and who is unable or unwilling to return to, and is unable or unwilling to avail himself or herself of the protection of, that country because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion." Immigration and Nationality Act § 101(a)(42)(A) / 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A)

U.S. Law — Right to Apply
"Any alien who is physically present in the United States or who arrives in the United States (whether or not at a designated port of arrival and including an alien who is brought to the United States after having been interdicted in international or United States waters), irrespective of such alien's status, may apply for asylum..." INA § 208(a)(1) / 8 U.S.C. § 1158(a)(1)

International Law — Definition of Refugee
A refugee is any person who, "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion, is outside the country of his nationality and is unable or, owing to such fear, is unwilling to avail himself of the protection of that country." 1951 Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees, Art. 1A(2), United Nations

International Law — Non-Refoulement
"No Contracting State shall expel or return ('refouler') a refugee in any manner whatsoever to the frontiers of territories where his life or freedom would be threatened on account of his race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion." 1951 Refugee Convention, Art. 33(1). The U.S. acceded to the 1967 Protocol (which incorporates the 1951 definition) in 1968.

Standard of Proof — Supreme Court
The Court held that "well-founded fear" requires only a reasonable possibility of persecution — as low as a 10% probability. This is intentionally lower than the "clear probability" standard required for withholding of removal. INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421 (1987)

The United States has a law that says anyone physically present may apply for asylum. It has a system that takes 4.3 years to decide. It has 557 judges for 3.8 million cases. The law says cases should be resolved in six months.

This is not primarily a debate about who should be allowed into the country. It is a question about whether the government should do what the law says. If the law is wrong, Congress should change it honestly — not leave the executive to override it through administrative action.

Seven Structural Fixes

1. Statutory Right to Hearing

Problem: Judges instructed to dismiss cases without hearings.

No asylum application dismissed without an evidentiary hearing, unless voluntarily withdrawn.

2. Court Independence

Problem: Immigration judges are DOJ employees subject to political directives.

Establish an independent Article I court, modeled on the U.S. Tax Court. Fixed terms. Removable only for cause.

3. Mandatory Staffing Floors

Problem: Judge count dropped from 735 to 557 while caseload grew.

Require a minimum judge-to-case ratio (e.g., 1:1,500). Mandatory appropriations when exceeded.

4. Processing Timeline Enforcement

Problem: 180-day requirement is unenforceable.

If not adjudicated within 365 days, applicants receive automatic work authorization. Incentivize efficiency.

5. Nationality-Blind Processing

Problem: Categorical bars based on country of origin, not individual assessment.

Eligibility determined by individual circumstances. No executive-imposed nationality-based bars without congressional authorization.

6. Fee Reform

Problem: Mandatory annual fees on applicants who can't work.

Waive fees below 200% poverty level. Eliminate recurring fees. One-time filing fee only.

7. Credible Fear Codification

Problem: Standard fluctuates with each administration.

Codify "significant possibility" at ≥10% probability, consistent with INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca (1987).

The core question: should reform bring the system into alignment with the law that exists, or should Congress amend the law to match the system that has emerged? Which is more honest? Which is more functional?

Community Kitchen & Open Table Act

Rebuilding the social infrastructure around food

When people can't afford to eat well, they don't just lose nutrition — they lose access to one of the most basic human activities: sitting down to a shared meal. Eating together is how communities form. It's how cooking knowledge passes between generations. It's how neighbors become something more than strangers.

Existing programs address food access (SNAP, WIC, food banks) and food availability (Healthy Food Financing Initiative). Neither addresses the social and educational infrastructure that makes access translate into good nutritional outcomes — what the WHO calls food utilization.

Three Components

👨‍🍳

Cooking Education

Free classes in budget-conscious meal prep, nutritional literacy, food safety, and cultural foodways.

🍽

Open Table Dinners

Twice-monthly community dinners. RSVP-based with 10% planned surplus. Communal seating. Not a soup kitchen — a dinner party.

🏪

Shared Kitchen Access

Subsidized commercial kitchen space for food entrepreneurs. Business incubator at marginal cost.

The "not a soup kitchen" framing is essential. Communal seating. Social time. Cultural menus. No solicitation of payment. The goal is a community dinner, not a charity meal — because the social function of shared meals is destroyed when the experience is reduced to standing in line for a tray.
Federal Investment

$150 million per year supports 100–125 community kitchens annually, or 500–625 over the authorization period. A modest investment relative to the $100+ billion annual SNAP budget. The program builds physical and social infrastructure that outlasts the grant period.

Eligible grantees include municipal governments, nonprofits, Tribal governments, school districts, and houses of worship. The 20% match can include in-kind contributions. Annual reporting covers attendance, food waste metrics, kitchen utilization, and sourcing data.

This is what positive-sum policy looks like: a program that feeds people, teaches skills, reduces waste, incubates businesses, and rebuilds community — all from the same investment.
